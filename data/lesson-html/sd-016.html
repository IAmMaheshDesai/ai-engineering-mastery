sd-016 · Capacity protection

Accept only work the system can usefully complete

Rate, concurrency, queueing, fairness, and overload response are separate controls.

Why a healthy fleet can fail under demand

An inference platform has enough accelerators for its ordinary workload. A customer sends a burst of long prompts while a downstream metadata service slows. Requests remain in flight longer, memory grows, timeouts trigger retries, and the system spends increasing effort on work that clients have already abandoned. Adding a requests-per-minute limit at the edge helps one dimension but does not bound prompt bytes, active sequences, or downstream concurrency. The engineering problem is to preserve useful completion and safety as demand exceeds a specific resource envelope.

Rate limiting constrains admitted work over time under a defined counting rule. Concurrency limiting constrains work simultaneously occupying a resource. Admission control decides whether a request may enter based on policy and capacity. Load shedding rejects or removes work to protect useful service under overload. A queue buffers timing differences but can also hide overload until deadlines become impossible. These mechanisms complement each other; none is a universal substitute.

Define the protected resource and unit. Requests per second can protect an API parser, bytes per second a network or ingestion path, input tokens a prefill workload, active sequences a memory budget, and provider calls an external quota. A business spend limit is another contract, often requiring durable accounting rather than a transient traffic meter. Do not let a single “token” name obscure whether you mean limiter credits, language-model tokens, or money.

Your OCI/FSDR experience maps directly to admission under failure. Recovery commands must retain a budget while telemetry, retries, and background reconciliation surge. That does not mean every recovery request can bypass capacity checks or authorization. The examples here are hypothetical designs connecting cloud orchestration to AI systems, not claims about OCI internals or your unprovided work history.

Define the promise and rejection

A contractual limit, an abuse threshold, and an adaptive overload cap may all apply to one request. Return a reason that reflects the failed condition without leaking sensitive internal state. A tenant exceeding its agreed allowance differs from a service temporarily unable to honor normal demand. Retry guidance should account for whether capacity can plausibly return before the client's deadline. An immediate retry loop is not a useful response to a finite resource.

Admission should occur early enough to avoid expensive work, but after enough authenticated classification to choose the correct policy. Rejecting before TLS or identity can protect cheap resources while a later tenant-aware decision protects shared compute. Parsing an enormous body merely to discover that it is forbidden wastes capacity; enforce size and transport limits at an appropriate earlier layer. Security and capacity defenses should agree on which fields are trusted.

How I would explain this in an interview: “I first choose the resource and guarantee. A rate budget controls arrivals, a concurrency budget controls occupied capacity, and a deadline-aware queue decides whether waiting is useful. Under overload, I shed work before it consumes the bottleneck, while preserving authorization and protected recovery capacity.” Pause and classify which controls protect an API gateway, a GPU decoder, and a destructive cloud tool.

Chapter summary

Protect concrete resources with explicit units and rejection semantics. A quota is not a concurrency bound, and a queue is not evidence that accepted work can meet its deadline.

02 · Token buckets and bursts

Separate sustained rate from permitted burst

A bucket's capacity and refill rate encode different parts of the traffic envelope.

Derive the state transition

A token bucket holds up to capacity B credits and refills at rate r credits per second. At authoritative time t, update the balance to the smaller of B and the previous balance plus r times nonnegative elapsed time. Admit cost c only if the updated balance is at least c, then subtract c atomically with the decision. A full bucket permits an initial burst; refill permits sustained traffic. Under the standard model, total admitted cost over an interval of length Δ is bounded by B + rΔ, subject to endpoint conventions and the initial state.

Use a monotonic time source within the relevant authority or a carefully defined shared-clock protocol. A backward wall-clock jump must not mint or remove credits unpredictably. A distributed implementation cannot safely perform read-balance, compute, then write in separate unprotected operations. The conditional decision and balance update need one supported atomic scope. Fixed-point units can avoid floating-point drift when precise accounting matters.

A leaky-bucket shaper can pace departures, while a meter can decide eligibility without itself queueing. GCRA expresses a related conformance envelope through a theoretical arrival time and tolerance. These are useful alternatives when a timestamp representation or smoothing policy fits better. The label alone does not tell clients whether excess work is delayed or rejected. Define both the traffic envelope and the treatment of nonconforming requests.

Worked example 1: weighted requests consume the bucket

Initial state: a tenant has bucket capacity 20 credits, refill rate 5 credits per second, and a full balance at time zero. The objective is to permit short bursts while bounding sustained resource use. At time zero, three requests cost 8, 7, and 6 credits. The first two leave balances 12 and 5. The third is rejected because six exceeds five. A rejection does not secretly reserve or consume extra credits unless that is an explicitly different policy.

At time 0.4 seconds, two credits have refilled, giving seven. A cost-six request is now admitted, leaving one. If a client asks how long until a cost-nine request could pass with no intervening work, the deficit is eight and the refill rate is five, so the lower-complexity wait is 1.6 seconds. Other admitted work or additional resource constraints can make actual admission later. Retry guidance is not a reservation of future capacity.

Intermediate state includes balance and last-accounted time, updated under one authority. Failure variation: two gateways both read balance seven and independently admit cost six. Without an atomic update, both succeed and spend twelve from seven. Central serialization, partitioned ownership, or exclusive leased credits can prevent that race under their contracts. Merely placing the balance in a shared cache is not enough.

Final outcome under the serial bucket is an explainable weighted traffic envelope. Production implication: choose costs from actual resource demand where possible, bound maximum request cost, and decide how estimates reconcile with actual work. A request whose cost exceeds capacity B can never pass this bucket without a separate route or policy change. Interview takeaway: show balance transitions and the exact atomic decision instead of only naming token bucket.

Estimated and actual cost

For generation, input size may be known while output length is uncertain. Reserve an approved upper bound or a defensible estimate, enforce execution limits, and reconcile under a repeat-safe accounting identity. Returning unused credits after completion needs protection against duplicate refunds. If a failed worker consumed real compute, blindly refunding the full reservation understates usage. Separate a contractual spend ledger from a short-lived rate meter when their durability and correction requirements differ.

Chapter summary

Capacity controls burst and refill controls sustained rate. Weighted admission needs atomic state transitions, trustworthy time, and a clear contract for estimated work and reconciliation.

03 · Windows and counting error

A window definition changes the guarantee

Boundary bursts and approximations are product semantics, not minor implementation details.

Fixed windows, sliding logs, and approximate counters

A fixed-window counter admits up to L units in each aligned interval. It is simple and compact, but two adjacent windows can create a large short burst near the boundary. A sliding log retains timestamps of admitted events and counts those within the last interval. That can provide a more literal rolling-window rule at the cost of memory and cleanup proportional to recent activity. Weighted events and distributed timestamps add further state.

A sliding-window counter approximates the rolling interval using current and prior bucket counts with a weight for overlap. It is cheaper than retaining every timestamp but has an error envelope depending on event distribution. Do not describe it as exact when traffic can be concentrated within the earlier bucket. State whether the business needs a strict rolling guarantee, a smooth sustained envelope, or a coarse abuse cap. The simplest acceptable mechanism depends on that answer.

Deduplication and counting are separate decisions. A client retry may consume another request allowance even if the business operation is idempotent, because parsing and lookup still cost resources. A durable business quota may instead count one logical action. Choose the identity and accounting stage explicitly. Attackers can exploit ambiguous rules if one request fans out into many downstream operations while only the outer call is counted.

Worked example 2: the fixed-minute boundary

Initial state: a fixed-window policy permits 100 requests per aligned minute. A client sends 100 at 12:00:59.9 and 100 more at 12:01:00.1. The objective is to understand the actual guarantee. Each aligned minute contains only 100, so the fixed-window rule accepts both batches. Yet 200 requests arrive within 0.2 seconds. A backend unable to absorb that burst can fail despite the client obeying the advertised aligned-minute policy.

If the intended contract is no more than 100 in any trailing sixty seconds, an exact sliding-log decision at the second batch sees the previous 100 and rejects or defers. If the intended contract allows a burst of 100 with a sustained refill near 100 per minute, a token bucket expresses that envelope differently. It may admit a small amount of replenished work after 0.2 seconds, not a fresh full hundred. These are distinct policies, not interchangeable optimizations.

Failure variation: a service uses approximate counters but documents an exact rolling limit. Adversarially clustered timestamps can exceed the intended bound. Either select an implementation whose guarantee matches the contract or publish the permissible approximation. Intermediate state and endpoint rules matter for events exactly at expiry; define whether the interval is open or closed at its lower bound and use one consistent time authority.

Final outcome is a mechanism chosen for the declared traffic envelope. Production implication: load-test synchronized clients and time boundaries, not only uniform averages. Add a separate concurrency or burst cap if an aligned-window business allowance must remain. Interview takeaway: demonstrate the boundary counterexample and ask what the customer actually expects before choosing a more complex algorithm.

Cardinality and abuse resistance

A per-user limiter can itself be attacked with unbounded unique identities or descriptor values. Authenticate before allocating expensive tenant state where possible, cap descriptor cardinality, expire state safely, and keep aggregate protections for unauthenticated traffic. Hashing keys does not bound their count. Avoid storing raw secrets or prompts as limiter descriptors. Policy should distinguish tenant, route, model class, and operation cost without creating an uncontrolled memory surface.

Chapter summary

Fixed, rolling, and bucket envelopes differ. Match implementation accuracy and identity to the contract, and protect the limiter's own memory and processing budget.

04 · Distributed enforcement

Global limits require global accounting or bounded delegation

Independent local buckets multiply capacity unless their shares are coordinated.

Local speed and global authority

A local limiter is fast and can protect each proxy before expensive shared checks. But giving every proxy the full global allowance multiplies aggregate admission by the number of active proxies. A global decision service can serialize a tenant budget across the fleet, at the cost of latency, load, and a new dependency. Combining coarse local protection with a global contract can reduce pressure, provided the two layers' units and failure behavior are explicit.

Exclusive credit allocation delegates a bounded share to each gateway. The central authority deducts credits before issuing a unique grant, and a gateway spends its grant locally. If the authority does not reissue unaccounted credits, global spend can remain bounded even through disconnection. The cost is stranded capacity when one gateway is idle or dies. Expiring and reclaiming grants safely needs a protocol preventing the old holder from continuing to spend after reallocation.

Approximate distributed counting offers another tradeoff: gateways report usage periodically while local decisions proceed. The global limit can overshoot. Derive a conservative error bound from reporting interval, per-gateway local rate, burst allowance, and in-flight work. Average observed error is not a worst-case guarantee. If the contract is strict money or scarce capacity, unbounded approximate overshoot may be unacceptable.

Worked example 3: a reporting-delay error budget

Initial state: ten gateways may each admit up to 100 requests per second under a local ceiling, and report to a shared counter every two seconds. Ignore additional local burst capacity for the first calculation. The objective is to bound admissions that can be unseen when the shared counter reaches a global threshold. Under synchronized worst-case reporting delay, each gateway can have up to 200 recently admitted requests not reflected centrally, totaling 2,000 across ten gateways.

This is a conservative teaching bound for the stated fixed-rate assumptions, not an automatic property of a particular product. If each gateway also has an unreported burst allowance of fifty, include up to another 500. In-flight decisions, reporting retries, and gateway count changes may add exposure. A global limit of 10,000 requests with a possible 2,500 extra is a materially different contract from an exact 10,000 cap.

For exclusive grants instead, suppose the authority has 1,000 credits and issues one hundred to each of ten gateways, deducting all grants atomically from the remaining pool. A disconnected gateway can spend only its remaining share. Aggregate spend stays within 1,000 under the grant protocol, but a hot gateway may reject while another holds idle credits. Intermediate state includes issued, spent, returned, and uncertain grants, each identified durably enough for the contract.

Failure variation: the authority times out issuing a grant and retries with a new identity, or reclaims an expired grant while the old gateway still spends. Either can double-issue rights unless identity and exclusion are enforced. Final outcome is a deliberate choice between strict bounded delegation, central per-request decisions, and explicitly approximate enforcement. Interview takeaway: quantify the error or stranded-capacity cost instead of calling a distributed counter “global” without a failure model.

Failure-open and failure-closed by operation

If the global limiter is unavailable, a service may use a bounded emergency allowance, reject new work, or continue under a documented weaker contract. Decide per operation. A read-only public endpoint might accept bounded risk; a prepaid spending limit or destructive recovery action may require fail-closed behavior. A local emergency budget must not renew indefinitely while disconnected if it is intended to bound exposure. Observability should identify the active mode and the amount of authority delegated.

Chapter summary

Local enforcement protects local resources; global guarantees require shared accounting, exclusive rights, or an explicit approximation bound. Failure modes and grant lifecycle determine the real promise.

05 · Concurrency and occupied capacity

A constant arrival rate can become overload when service slows

Protect active work and release credits only when resources are really freed.

Concurrency is rate multiplied by time under stable averages

For a stable boundary, Little's Law relates average in-system population L, arrival rate λ, and average time W through L = λW. If the boundary is only active execution, use execution time and active work. If it includes waiting, include the queue. The equation describes compatible averages; it does not give a p99 bound or guarantee stability. It is useful because slowdown increases occupied work even when request rate stays unchanged.

A semaphore-like concurrency limiter admits only while active weight is below a capacity budget. Acquire before occupying the protected resource and release after actual completion or verified termination. A client timeout does not prove the backend stopped. If the system returns the permit while compute still runs, it can over-admit. If a crashed worker never releases a permit, capacity can leak. The lifecycle needs ownership, expiry, and reconciliation appropriate to the actual runtime.

Weighted concurrency can represent memory, active language-model sequences, or estimated work, but estimates require validation. A request with a large prompt can consume much more memory than a tiny one. Separate hard physical safety from soft scheduling estimates. A rate meter may permit a request while the concurrency meter rejects it, and that is not a contradiction: the two protect different dimensions.

Worked example 4: downstream latency increases tenfold

Initial state: a service admits 200 requests per second, with average active execution time of 0.1 seconds. Stable average active population is about twenty. A downstream slowdown raises average execution time to one second while arrivals remain at 200. The objective is to prevent memory and connection exhaustion. The corresponding stable-average active demand becomes 200, a tenfold increase without any arrival-rate change.

If the service has a tested active-capacity budget of eighty equivalent requests, the old rate policy alone cannot protect it. Under the simplified one-second execution model, eighty active slots can sustain about eighty completions per second. Remaining offered demand must be queued within a useful bound, rejected, or degraded through an approved path. An unbounded queue moves the resource problem into waiting memory and client deadlines.

Intermediate state during slowdown includes growing active work, delayed completions, and possibly retries. Acquire and release must be associated with a stable attempt or request identity so duplicate completion events do not release twice. Failure variation: a client cancels, but the remote operation continues for five more seconds. Its permit remains occupied until the protected work stops or the system safely accounts for it elsewhere. Otherwise the apparent eighty-slot cap can hide more than eighty real operations.

Final outcome is bounded active work and explicit overload behavior, not preservation of all offered traffic. Production implication: observe active permits, actual runtime work, permit leaks, cancellations, and queue age. Adaptive concurrency may respond to latency signals, but it must be tested for instability and noisy measurements. Interview takeaway: rate protects arrivals; concurrency protects occupied resources and must follow the real execution lifecycle.

Bulkheads isolate different bottlenecks

Use separate pools for costly routes, tenants, provider dependencies, or priority classes when one can otherwise exhaust shared capacity. A single global semaphore can let slow optional calls block critical state reads. Too many rigid partitions can strand capacity, so define borrowing and reclaim policy carefully. The protected recovery class in a DR platform needs an authenticated budget and a path independent of ordinary retry storms, not an unlimited bypass around every control.

Chapter summary

Slowdown increases concurrency demand even at a fixed rate. Bound active resources, reconcile permit lifecycle with real execution, and isolate bottlenecks so optional work cannot consume every critical slot.

06 · Deadline-aware queues and shedding

Reject work that cannot still be useful

Queue length is not the same as remaining service time.

Waiting is an explicit admission choice

A bounded queue can absorb a short burst while service catches up. Its useful bound depends on deadlines, resource cost, and service variability, not merely available memory. If a request has 200 milliseconds left and known unavoidable work already exceeds that, accepting it may waste resources and worsen everyone else's latency. Rejecting early can preserve useful completions even though raw acceptance decreases.

Propagate a remaining deadline budget across hops rather than restarting a full timeout at each service. A downstream stage should account for elapsed upstream time and return promptly when useful completion is no longer plausible. Clocks and transport conventions need care; a duration budget and local monotonic accounting can avoid some wall-clock ambiguity. Do not trust a client to grant itself infinite priority or manipulate deadline metadata without policy.

FIFO is simple but can place short useful work behind long tasks. Separate classes, earliest-deadline policies, or cost-aware scheduling can improve outcomes under stated assumptions, but can also starve large work or be gamed by inaccurate estimates. Define fairness and maximum waiting. Shedding can target low-priority, expired, or expensive optional work, but must not silently drop a command for which durable responsibility was already accepted without a declared terminal outcome.

Worked example 5: the queue already consumes the deadline

Initial state: a simplified executor completes 500 equivalent requests per second and has 100 equivalent requests ahead in a FIFO queue. A new request has 250 milliseconds of remaining end-to-end budget and needs an estimated 80 milliseconds after leaving the queue. The objective is to decide whether queueing is useful under a deterministic approximation. Nominal waiting is 100 divided by 500, or 200 milliseconds. Waiting plus estimated execution is 280 milliseconds, already above the remaining 250.

The service rejects or uses an approved degraded path rather than adding doomed work. If only fifty requests are ahead, nominal wait is 100 milliseconds and total is 180, leaving seventy milliseconds of arithmetic slack. That does not establish a tail guarantee: service times, scheduling, network, and estimation error may consume it. Admission should use a conservative measured policy and keep uncertainty visible.

Intermediate state includes queue weight, oldest age, available service, and remaining request budget. Failure variation: records vary widely in cost, so one hundred entries represent far more or less work than expected. Track an appropriate work estimate and hard resource limits rather than count alone. Another variation is a request canceled while waiting; remove or mark it so it does not occupy queue and execution capacity unnecessarily.

Final outcome is a deadline-aware choice aligned with useful completion. Production implication: measure expired-before-start, shed reasons, accepted completion ratio, and wasted execution after client abandonment. Do not optimize a dashboard by returning fast errors while concealing which contract failed, but do distinguish controlled shedding from an uncontrolled outage. Interview takeaway: capacity protection is about successful useful work, not accepting the maximum number of requests.

Retry policy participates in the queue

Return bounded retry guidance where meaningful and use exponential backoff with jitter in cooperating clients. Retry only when the operation is safe and enough deadline remains. A proxy retry and an application retry can multiply attempts if their budgets are independent. Propagate an attempt budget or account for retries at an authoritative boundary. A client-generated idempotency key can protect business effects, but repeated parsing, lookup, and failed attempts still consume capacity.

Chapter summary

Use remaining time and estimated queued work to decide whether waiting can help. Count attempts and cancellation costs, and reject early enough to preserve useful capacity.

07 · Fairness, reserves, and adaptation

Protect recovery without turning priority into privilege escalation

A fair overload policy must state entitlements, borrowing, and failure behavior.

Hierarchical policy

Apply budgets at several scopes when needed: global resource, region or pool, tenant, route, and operation class. A tenant must satisfy all relevant hard conditions, but the decision should avoid partially charging budgets when a later check rejects. An atomic multi-budget operation is one option within a supported scope. Reservation and repeat-safe release can work with more complexity. Independent decrements followed by best-effort refunds can leak or over-refund rights under crashes.

Weighted sharing can allocate capacity proportional to entitlement while per-tenant maxima limit abuse. Borrowing idle capacity improves utilization, but borrowed work needs a reclaim or nonrenewal policy when owners demand their share. Protected recovery capacity can be held aside or lent only to safely preemptible work. Define how long reclaim takes, because an idle-looking reserve occupied by a long nonpreemptible task may not meet an emergency start objective.

Priority should be assigned from authenticated policy, not an arbitrary request header. A recovery control plane may authorize a narrow emergency action class with its own audit and resource budget. That class still needs tenant authorization, fencing, and provider limits. A high-priority request cannot justify conflicting resource ownership or unsafe duplicate effects.

Worked example 6: reserve and borrow under emergency demand

Initial state: a control-plane executor has 100 equivalent active slots. Twenty are reserved for authorized recovery operations and eighty for ordinary management. Recovery demand is normally zero. The objective is to use idle capacity without losing the ability to start up to twenty emergency operations quickly. If ordinary work borrows the twenty slots, it must be safely preemptible or finish within the reclaim deadline. Lending to unbounded external calls would invalidate the reserve promise.

At incident time, fifteen recovery operations arrive. If fifteen borrowed tasks can stop safely within two seconds, the platform reclaims those slots and admits recovery under the agreed startup budget. Five reserve slots remain for additional recovery, while ordinary work uses its eighty base slots plus any explicitly still-lendable capacity. The accounting must not count preemption requested as preemption completed when the resource is still occupied.

Failure variation: borrowed tasks hold noncancelable provider operations for thirty seconds. The claimed two-second emergency start cannot be met. The design must either keep reserve idle, use a different independent pool, accept a longer objective, or change the lending contract. Another variation is an attacker labeling every request recovery. Policy authentication and audit prevent the priority class from becoming an abuse bypass.

Final outcome is an explicit utilization-versus-reclaim tradeoff. Production implication: monitor reserve occupancy, borrowed-work age, reclaim latency, emergency rejection, and provider bottlenecks. A reserve of executor slots does not help if all database connections or credentials are shared and saturated. Interview takeaway: protect the entire critical path and show that the mechanism restoring reserve actually releases resources.

Adaptive controls and feedback

An adaptive limiter can reduce concurrency when latency or errors indicate saturation and cautiously increase it when conditions improve. Delayed signals, workload changes, and interacting autoscalers can cause oscillation. For example, raising concurrency to clear a queue can increase latency, causing more active work and retries. Separate timescales, bound changes, use hysteresis where appropriate, and test the closed loop under realistic delays. A machine-learned estimator still needs a safe fallback and hard physical limits.

Scaling capacity is not instantaneous. Loading a large model or provisioning a suitable GPU pool may take longer than the current deadline. Admission protects the service during that lag. Do not count requested replicas as ready capacity. A capacity signal should represent verified usable throughput for the actual workload, including failure-state reserve and routing skew.

Chapter summary

Fairness and reserves need authenticated hierarchy and real reclaim semantics. Adaptive controls must remain stable with delayed observations and capacity changes, while hard safety limits stay enforceable.

08 · AI and public infrastructure context

Protect token, memory, and tool budgets independently

One outer API request can create many different downstream costs.

Inference cost and unknown output

LLM requests vary in input length, output length, model, batching, and cache reuse. A fixed request count can admit a workload far outside the tested GPU envelope. Track known input and bound requested output, then use model-specific admission and active-memory accounting. A token budget is still an approximation of hardware work; prefill and decode have different characteristics. Do not claim that all tokens cost the same amount of time or memory across models.

Prompt caching can reduce some prefill work while leaving new output generation. A policy may account for cached and uncached work differently, but must use the actual provider or runtime contract. Avoid promising a static cost discount based on an assumed cache hit that is not guaranteed. For a strict spend ceiling, reserve enough under policy and reconcile a durable usage record without duplicate refunds. The transient execution limiter and financial ledger can have different persistence requirements.

Anthropic's public rate-limit documentation provides an example of service-configured usage constraints. NVIDIA Triton documentation provides a different layer: resource-based scheduling of model instances. Neither alone establishes a full multi-tenant global admission design. The lesson's buckets, delegated rights, and reserve counts are hypothetical and should not be presented as those companies' private enforcement implementations.

Agents multiply requests and effects

An agent may create several model calls, retrieval queries, and external tool actions from one user request. Set budgets for logical run, tenant, tool, and provider, and define whether child work inherits or reserves from the parent's allowance. A runaway planning loop should not consume unlimited tokens because the outer API request passed once. Persist budget state when a durable workflow can resume after a crash, and ensure retries cannot reset the allowance.

Effectful tools also need safety identity. Rate limiting reduces volume; it does not make a duplicate cloud action safe. A provider may return a rate rejection after some earlier action already succeeded, so the next attempt must preserve business identity and reconcile uncertain outcomes. Human approval does not imply unlimited execution capacity or authorization to bypass provider restrictions.

For an OCI-style recovery assistant, read-only diagnostics can have a lower-priority or sampled budget while approved failover actions use a protected class. Reconciliation queries need their own reserve so the platform can resolve unknown actions during a storm. The agent may suggest an action, but deterministic policy and current authority decide whether it can run. These are original architecture connections, not claims about an existing OCI agent implementation.

Public evidence and architectural lessons

OpenAI's PostgreSQL scaling article discusses overload protection at several layers, providing public motivation for isolating backend resources. Meta's ZGateway account describes a managed proxy as an admission and operational control point. Google's SRE overload guidance discusses protecting useful service, and AWS's load-shedding material supplies additional primary operational context. These sources justify studying layered controls; they do not establish the invented rates or a universal company SLO.

Envoy documents local and global rate-limiting mechanisms. A local filter can shield the global decision service, while a global policy still needs an explicit failure and accounting contract. Choose placement based on cost, trust, and protected resources rather than assuming the gateway is the only layer. How I would explain this in an interview: “I enforce the cheapest safe limits early, then refine by tenant and resource as evidence becomes available. Every downstream bottleneck retains a guardrail.”

Chapter summary

AI admission is multi-dimensional and often hierarchical. Preserve budget identity across child work and retries, and keep external-effect safety separate from traffic protection.

09 · Production admission architecture

Design a global AI capacity protection service

A complete hypothetical design combining tenant policy, delegated rate, active capacity, and deadlines.

Requirements, interfaces, and durable authority

Design a platform with one million edge requests per second, several model and tool pools, and tenant-specific entitlements. Assume global contractual budgets need a stated bounded or strict accounting mode, while each regional execution pool needs hard active-capacity protection. Recovery operations have an authenticated reserve. The platform must reject overload early, preserve safe existing work, and avoid turning the limiter into the largest new bottleneck. These numbers are workload assumptions for design, not claims about a target company's deployment.

Use cheap local transport and request-size guards before expensive parsing, then authenticated tenant and route classification. An admission coordinator evaluates policy revision, tenant rate rights, resource class, remaining deadline, and current execution capacity. Global budget authority issues bounded exclusive grants where the contract supports delegation. Regional executors enforce weighted active permits. A bounded scheduler queues only work that can still plausibly meet its class policy. Usage reconciliation records actual cost and releases reservations repeatably.

An admission request includes authenticated tenant, logical request identity, operation class, model or provider, known input weight, approved output bound, remaining deadline, and policy revision. Return admitted with a reservation identity, deferred under an explicit asynchronous contract, or rejected with a scoped reason and meaningful retry guidance. A completion message contains the reservation identity and observed usage or termination evidence. Duplicate messages must not mint refunds or release active permits twice.

Persist policy versions, contractual usage where required, credit grants, grant identities and uncertain states, and durable workflow budgets. Keep transient high-frequency active accounting near the resource when its recovery contract permits, but reconstruct or conservatively reconcile after failure. Financial spend limits may require stronger persistence than a local abuse meter. A policy cache can improve availability only while its expiry, revocation, and delegated authority remain valid.

Execution, refunds, and failure recovery

Normal flow first rejects unauthenticated or oversized work under coarse rules. The tenant decision then reserves the applicable rate or cost authority and an execution permit through a supported atomic or recoverable protocol. If several budgets cannot be checked atomically, define partial reservation states and repeat-safe release. The request enters a bounded deadline-aware queue or starts immediately. Completion reconciles actual usage and releases only resources known to be free.

If the global budget service fails, gateways spend only valid remaining grants or use the documented fallback mode. They do not renew an emergency allowance forever while disconnected. If a gateway crashes after receiving a grant, the authority treats its unreturned rights as spent or uncertain until safe reclamation. If a worker disappears after consuming compute, the system cannot infer zero cost from missing completion. Reconcile execution evidence and bound future admission conservatively.

A model pool slowdown reduces active completion capacity. The concurrency guard limits occupied work while admission rejects or defers excess. Autoscaling requests additional compatible capacity, but the controller does not count it until readiness is verified. A region loss may shift traffic and invalidate per-region budgets; grants and reservations must follow the declared ownership and residency policy. Do not recreate the full global allowance independently in every surviving region.

Scalability, security, operations, and alternatives

At one million requests per second, a remote synchronous decision per request can be expensive and become a shared failure surface. Local prefilters and delegated credits reduce decision load. If each grant covers one hundred accepted units, the nominal grant rate can be around ten thousand per second under uniform full utilization, before churn and retries. That approximation is not a benchmark or a guarantee: tenant skew, small grants, and strict policies can increase it. Size the authority and its metadata from measured distributions.

Authenticate priority and accounting descriptors. Bind grants and permits to tenant, gateway or executor identity, policy epoch, and scope. Reject forged completion/refund messages. Bound descriptor cardinality and request body size. Never let a client claim another tenant's quota key or emergency class. Audit policy changes and emergency overrides, and define who may accept a weaker enforcement mode during an incident.

Observe admitted and rejected work by reason, active weighted permits, actual resource use, queue age, expired-before-start, retry amplification, grant utilization, stranded rights, uncertain refunds, and reserve reclaim latency. Track useful completion rather than acceptance alone. A runbook includes freezing policy rollout, reducing concurrency, pausing replay or background work, isolating an abusive tenant, restoring budget authority, and reclaiming leaked permits with evidence.

Compare a central exact decision service, exclusive grant delegation, approximate periodic counting, and local-only protection. Choose per resource and business contract. Introduce shadow evaluation before enforcing a new policy, then canary a bounded traffic class. Shadow decisions must not consume real rights or double-charge usage. Keep compatibility across policy epochs so rolling gateways do not interpret the same grant differently. The architecture defense names error bounds, authority, and failure behavior rather than claiming one algorithm solves every layer.

Chapter summary

A production admission service joins policy, rights, occupied resources, deadlines, and reconciliation. Its failure mode must preserve both the protected resource and the accounting promise.

10 · Executable bucket and validation

Make the accounting transition testable

A serial model demonstrates the traffic envelope without claiming distributed atomicity.

Token-bucket state and assumptions

The Python example uses a controllable time argument so tests can reproduce the weighted bucket trace exactly. It stores capacity, refill rate, balance, and last-accounted time. A request refills up to capacity, checks cost, and subtracts only on acceptance. Backward time is rejected rather than silently minting credits. The model uses floating-point values for readable arithmetic; production may use fixed-point units and a specified rounding rule.

This implementation is serial and in-memory. It has no thread safety, persistence, global grants, refund protocol, or external resource enforcement. In production, the refill-check-decrement transition must be one supported atomic operation or protected by an equivalent single authority. Translating it into an unlocked read and write is not correct. The tests establish the intended local state sequence, not a proof of a deployed service's distributed behavior.

PYTHON · executable standard-library model
class Bucket:
    # Serial teaching model: production state transition must be atomic.
    def __init__(self, capacity, rate, now=0.0):
        if capacity <= 0 or rate <= 0:
            raise ValueError('positive capacity and rate required')
        self.capacity, self.rate = capacity, rate
        self.balance, self.last = float(capacity), now

    def admit(self, cost, now):
        if cost <= 0 or now < self.last:
            raise ValueError('invalid cost or backward time')
        self.balance = min(self.capacity, self.balance + self.rate * (now - self.last))
        self.last = now
        if cost > self.balance:
            return False
        self.balance -= cost
        return True

b = Bucket(20, 5)
assert b.admit(8, 0) is True
assert b.admit(7, 0) is True
assert b.admit(6, 0) is False
assert b.admit(6, 0.4) is True
assert abs(b.balance - 1) < 1e-9
assert 10 * 100 * 2 == 2000
assert 10 * 50 == 500
assert 200 * 0.1 == 20
assert 200 * 1 == 200
assert 100 / 500 + 0.080 > 0.250
assert 50 / 500 + 0.080 < 0.250
print('weighted bucket transitions and capacity calculations passed')

The assertions accept costs eight and seven at time zero, reject six, then accept six after 0.4 seconds of refill. They also verify the reporting-delay exposure, active-demand increase, and deadline arithmetic. Each local bucket operation is constant time with constant state. A fleet-wide per-tenant implementation uses memory proportional to retained active descriptors; that cardinality and its garbage collection need protection. An exact sliding log has a different recent-event memory cost.

Extend the model with idempotent reservation and refund identities, then write the crash cases before adding a network. A duplicate completion must not restore credits twice. A timed-out admission response must not cause a new unrelated reservation on retry. Explain which charge is for attempted work, which is for completed resource use, and which is a durable business spend. The exercise is to make accounting semantics precise enough to test.

11 · Original interview practice

Defend rejection as part of reliable service

Original Senior Staff questions, not confirmed company interview reports.

Twelve capacity-protection challenges

1. Why is a requests-per-second limit insufficient for inference?

Evaluation: resource modeling. Prompt length, output length, model, memory, cache reuse, and active duration vary. Use relevant weighted rate and concurrency controls with hard execution bounds. Follow-up: output length is unknown. Reserve under policy and reconcile repeatably. Common mistake: treating all requests as equal cost. Tradeoff: richer estimates improve protection but add uncertainty and accounting complexity.

2. How does a token bucket permit bursts without unlimited sustained load?

Evaluation: algorithm mechanics. Capacity B bounds accumulated credits and refill r bounds continued admission; under the model an interval admits at most B + rΔ. Show atomic refill-check-decrement and trustworthy time. Follow-up: two gateways race on one balance. Use a real authority or exclusive rights. Common mistake: naming the algorithm without state transitions. Tradeoff: a larger burst allowance can overload a downstream resource without an additional concurrency guard.

3. What is wrong with “100 per minute” as a complete contract?

Evaluation: window semantics. Fixed aligned windows can accept 200 near the boundary, unlike an exact rolling rule. Decide whether the customer needs aligned allowance, rolling count, or a rate-plus-burst envelope. Follow-up: use approximate counters. State error assumptions. Common mistake: calling every minute-based meter equivalent. Tradeoff: exact recent-event tracking costs more state than compact approximations.

4. Does a local limit of 100 on each of ten gateways enforce global 100?

Evaluation: distributed scope. No; independent full allowances can multiply aggregate admission. Allocate disjoint shares, use a shared decision boundary, or publish an approximation contract. Follow-up: gateways scale to twenty. Recompute authority rather than retaining full local copies. Common mistake: calling a fleet of local buckets global. Tradeoff: central accuracy costs coordination; delegation can strand rights.

5. What overshoot can periodic reporting hide?

Evaluation: error bounds. Under ten gateways at 100/s and two seconds reporting delay, up to 2,000 admissions can be unseen before extra bursts and in-flight effects. Add each exposure explicitly. Follow-up: fifty burst credits each. Include another 500 under the stated model. Common mistake: using average observed error as a guarantee. Tradeoff: smaller intervals reduce exposure but increase coordination traffic.

6. When the global limiter fails, should you fail open?

Evaluation: operation-specific risk. Decide by contractual strictness and protected resource. Spend valid bounded grants, use a documented limited fallback, or reject. An endlessly renewed emergency allowance is not bounded. Follow-up: prepaid spending or destructive action. Preserve strict authority where required. Common mistake: one universal availability answer. Tradeoff: conservative rejection protects budgets but reduces temporary service.

7. Why can constant arrival rate cause a concurrency incident?

Evaluation: occupied work. At 200/s, duration rising from 0.1 to one second changes average active demand from twenty to 200 under stable assumptions. Rate alone does not bound occupancy. Follow-up: eighty slots remain. Shed or queue within a useful deadline. Common mistake: blaming only traffic spikes. Tradeoff: concurrency caps protect resources while rejecting demand during slowdown.

8. Can you return a permit when the client times out?

Evaluation: lifecycle correctness. Only if the protected work has actually released its resource or another safe accounting boundary covers it. Remote work may continue. Bind permits to attempt identity and reconcile duplicate or lost completion. Follow-up: worker crashes. Explain leak detection and safe reclaim. Common mistake: equating caller abandonment with backend termination. Tradeoff: conservative permits can reduce capacity while preventing over-admission.

9. Should a request with 250 ms remaining join the example queue?

Evaluation: deadline reasoning. At 100 ahead and 500/s, nominal wait is 200 ms; adding 80 ms execution exceeds budget. Reject or use an approved alternative, then account for variance in real policies. Follow-up: costs vary widely. Use weighted work and conservative evidence. Common mistake: accepting because memory exists. Tradeoff: early rejection preserves useful work but needs honest client semantics.

10. Can ordinary work borrow emergency reserve?

Evaluation: reclaim contract. Only if borrowing preserves the emergency objective through safe preemption or bounded completion. A noncancelable thirty-second call cannot support a two-second reclaim promise. Follow-up: database connections are still shared. Protect the full critical path. Common mistake: counting requested cancellation as freed capacity. Tradeoff: idle reserve costs utilization but can provide predictable recovery.

11. Why can adaptive concurrency and autoscaling oscillate?

Evaluation: feedback delay. Both respond to delayed noisy signals, and raising concurrency can increase latency and in-flight work before capacity arrives. Bound changes, separate timescales, and validate the closed loop. Follow-up: model loading fails. Count verified ready capacity only. Common mistake: assuming more concurrency always clears backlog. Tradeoff: cautious adaptation may react slower but avoids instability.

12. How do you keep an agent from bypassing budgets after resume?

Evaluation: durable hierarchy. Persist run and child-work identities, reserve from tenant/tool/provider budgets, and make retries and refunds repeat-safe. A resumed workflow cannot reset its allowance. Priority and approval remain authenticated. Follow-up: a tool outcome is uncertain. Reconcile effects separately from traffic credits. Common mistake: counting only the outer API call. Tradeoff: durable accounting costs coordination but prevents runaway or duplicated spend.

Architecture defense: authority loss during regional evacuation

The interviewer removes the global budget service while a region evacuates and a retry storm begins. Clarify strict versus approximate budgets, remaining valid grants, residency, recovery priority, and actual survivor capacity. A strong solution spends only bounded delegated rights, protects local active resources, sheds expired or lower-value work, reserves reconciliation, and exposes the fallback mode. It does not give every surviving gateway a fresh global allowance. If the contract cannot be met, present a scoped availability reduction or an explicitly authorized risk decision rather than inventing unlimited safe admission.

Chapter summary

Strong answers name the unit, authority, atomic transition, error bound, and failure response. Reliable rejection is part of preserving useful service and contractual safety.

12 · Speaking and recall

Explain the admission decision at three depths

State what is protected, what can wait, and what must be rejected.

Thirty-second explanation

Rate limits bound arrivals over time; concurrency limits bound occupied resources; admission decides whether work may enter; load shedding protects useful service during overload. I choose explicit units, enforce atomic accounting, and match queues to remaining deadlines. Global guarantees need shared authority or bounded delegation. Recovery priority never bypasses authorization or real capacity.

Two-minute explanation

I would begin with the protected bottleneck and business promise. An edge parser, GPU memory pool, provider quota, and spend ledger need different units and durability. One requests-per-second limit cannot represent all of them. I would also distinguish a contractual tenant allowance from a dynamic overload response.

A token bucket gives a burst capacity and sustained refill rate. Its refill-check-decrement must be atomic under a trusted time model. A fixed-window counter has different boundary behavior, so I would not advertise it as an exact rolling limit. Across gateways, I would use a shared decision service, exclusive grants, or a clearly quantified approximation rather than giving each the full global budget.

Near execution, I would bound weighted active work and release permits only when resources are actually free. Slowdown increases concurrency even without increased arrivals. A bounded deadline-aware queue admits waiting only while completion remains useful. Retries, cancellation, and unknown external effects must participate in that lifecycle.

Finally, I would reserve authenticated recovery capacity across the full critical path, test limiter failure and delayed scaling, and observe useful completion rather than acceptance alone. The design is defensible when its accounting error, fallback exposure, and resource limits are explicit.

Five-minute explanation

Suppose we are protecting a global AI service during bursts and regional failures. I would first classify resources and contracts. Requests consume gateway work, prompt bytes and tokens affect inference, active sequences occupy memory, tool calls consume provider budgets, and money requires a durable ledger. A policy should say which of these is a hard invariant and which is a measured performance objective.

At the edge, I would enforce cheap transport and size protections before expensive parsing. After authentication, I would classify tenant, route, model, and priority from trusted policy. The admission decision would check the applicable rate or cost authority, active-capacity budget, and remaining deadline. It would return a reservation identity or a clear rejection/defer outcome, not a vague promise that queueing guarantees completion.

For a token bucket, I would show the balance transition: refill by elapsed time up to capacity, then atomically subtract accepted cost. Capacity allows a bounded burst while refill permits sustained work. I would handle backward time, rounding, maximum request cost, and duplicate accounting. If the business asks for a rolling-window promise, I would choose a matching exact or explicitly approximate implementation rather than silently use aligned windows.

Global enforcement introduces authority. A remote decision on every request is simple to reason about but can add latency and a shared bottleneck. Exclusive grants let gateways spend bounded rights locally, with stranded-capacity and safe-reclaim costs. Periodic reporting can reduce coordination but allows unseen admissions. I would derive the worst-case exposure from gateway count, local rate, report delay, burst, and in-flight work. Failure mode is part of that contract.

Execution needs a separate concurrency guard. At two hundred requests per second, a slowdown from a tenth of a second to one second increases average active demand from twenty to two hundred. A rate meter cannot prevent that occupancy growth. Permits remain held until the protected work ends, even if the client times out. Cancellation and worker loss require reconciliation so we neither leak capacity forever nor admit more work while old operations continue.

Queues should be bounded by useful work and time, not just memory. If estimated waiting plus required execution exceeds the remaining budget, early rejection can preserve other useful completions. Retries need shared budgets and jitter, and effectful operations need stable business identity. A rate rejection does not prove a previous tool attempt failed. For agents, child calls and resumed runs must inherit durable budget accounting.

I would preserve a protected recovery class only through authenticated policy and real resource reserve. Borrowing is allowed only if reclaim meets the emergency objective across shared dependencies. Adaptive concurrency and autoscaling would use bounded changes and verified ready capacity, with tests for delayed feedback. Finally, I would canary policy changes, audit overrides, and measure admitted completion, wasted work, queue expiry, grant uncertainty, and reserve reclaim. The goal is a controlled service boundary that remains honest and useful under overload.

Important concepts and final cheat sheet

  • Rate: admitted work over time.
  • Concurrency: occupied work at one time.
  • Admission: policy and capacity permission.
  • Shedding: rejecting work to protect useful service.
  • Bucket capacity: accumulated burst allowance.
  • Refill: sustained credit creation rate.
  • Atomic debit: one decision with its accounting update.
  • Delegated grant: exclusive bounded rights.
  • Approximation exposure: admissions not yet globally accounted.
  • Permit lifecycle: acquire through real resource release.
  • Deadline budget: remaining useful completion time.
  • Bulkhead: isolation of a capacity domain.
  • Recovery reserve: verified emergency critical-path capacity.
  • Closed-loop stability: safe behavior under delayed feedback.

Common mistakes are equating fixed and rolling windows, multiplying global allowance across replicas, returning permits on client timeout, and treating pending replicas as capacity. Replace each shortcut with a state transition and a failure bound. Useful phrases include “the accounting authority is,” “this grant bounds disconnected exposure,” and “the resource remains occupied until this evidence arrives.”

Whiteboard and speaking practice

In thirty seconds, distinguish rate from concurrency. In two minutes, show the fixed-window boundary burst and propose a matching alternative contract. In five minutes, defend global enforcement while the authority fails and a region evacuates. On a whiteboard, draw policy, rate rights, queue, active permits, actual execution, and refunds. Duplicate one grant and one completion, delay a cancellation, and state which invariant prevents over-admission or double credit.

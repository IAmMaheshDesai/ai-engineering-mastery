sd-014 · Queues and durable logs

Decouple work without hiding its contract

Buffering, ordering, replay, and completion are different promises.

Why the asynchronous boundary exists

A document service accepts uploads faster than an embedding pipeline can index them. Calling the GPU worker synchronously makes upload latency depend on accelerator availability. Adding a queue allows acceptance to be separated from execution, but it creates a new promise: accepted work must remain discoverable, eventually receive service under a sustainable load, and expose its actual completion state. A queue can decouple timing; it cannot eliminate capacity limits or make the user’s request complete by accepting a message.

A work queue usually distributes tasks among competing consumers, with acknowledgment or lease behavior controlling redelivery. A retained log preserves an ordered sequence of records within a defined scope, allowing consumers to maintain their own progress and replay history while retention permits. Real products can combine features, so choose by semantics rather than names. Ask whether several independent applications need the same history, whether a slow consumer must retain its own position, and whether completed tasks should remain available for rebuilding a projection.

For AI infrastructure, the distinction appears between executing one embedding task and feeding the same document-change history to indexing, audit, analytics, and governance consumers. For OCI-style recovery orchestration, a work queue can notify executors of eligible steps, while a durable journal remains the authority for plan state and external-effect identity. A notification is not itself permission to repeat a destructive action. These examples are hypothetical and do not assert proprietary OCI internals or personal accomplishments.

Define acceptance, durable append, delivery, processing, effect completion, and progress acknowledgment separately. A producer acknowledgment says something about the broker's write boundary. A consumer offset says something about recorded progress. Neither automatically proves that a customer-visible database update or tool call happened exactly once. The most important architecture diagram often marks these boundaries rather than merely drawing a broker between two services.

BoundaryWhat success establishesWhat it does not establish

Choose the unit of identity and replay

Give each logical event or task a stable identity scoped to the business domain. A broker record position identifies one append occurrence; a retried producer can create two occurrences of the same business event unless the producer/broker contract prevents it. Conversely, one record may intentionally lead to several different effects. Keep event identity, transport position, and effect identity distinct so deduplication does not discard legitimate work or replay harmful actions.

Record schema version, tenant, entity key, event identity, producer identity when useful, causation reference, and the minimum data needed to interpret the event. Avoid embedding unnecessary sensitive prompts or documents in a broadly replicated log. Large immutable payloads can live in controlled object storage with references and hashes, but retention and authorization must keep the references usable for the replay window. A reference to a deleted object is not a recoverable event.

How I would explain this in an interview: “I use asynchronous transport to separate acceptance from processing and to isolate bursts. Then I specify durability, ordering scope, replay, and effect completion independently. The queue or log is part of the workflow contract, not a substitute for it.” Pause and explain which parts of a document-indexing pipeline need replay and which parts need a single accepted logical outcome.

Chapter summary

Choose queue or log behavior by required observations and recovery. Stable business identities and explicit acknowledgment boundaries make retries and replay explainable.

02 · Partitioning and order

Order only what the business needs ordered

Parallelism and ordering meet at the partition key.

One sequence versus many sequences

A single ordered stream is easy to reason about but can become a throughput and availability bottleneck. Partitioning creates independent ordered sequences. A stable entity key can keep related events together, while unrelated entities progress concurrently. The guarantee is within that partition and protocol, not a universal order across all partitions. A timestamp field alone does not create global causal order when clocks differ and network delivery is delayed.

Choose the key from the invariant. Conversation events may need order per conversation, not per entire tenant. A recovery plan's steps may need order per execution or resource ownership group. If all records use a tenant key, one large tenant can become a hot partition. Splitting that tenant improves parallelism but requires a mechanism for any invariant spanning the new keys. Partitioning is therefore an application decision as much as a broker configuration.

Kafka's design documentation grounds the partitioned-log abstraction. The examples here teach general ordered-stream design rather than prescribing every version-specific client behavior. Producer retry ordering, idempotence, consumer assignment, and transactional visibility must be verified against the exact selected version and configuration. Do not advertise an ordering guarantee broader than the implementation actually supplies.

Worked example 1: a hot key limits scale

Initial state: four partitions each sustain 5,000 events per second under the tested record-size and durability workload. Total offered load is 12,000 events per second. Tenant H generates 7,000 events per second under one partition key; all other tenants generate 5,000 spread across the remaining partitions. The objective is stable ingestion without losing the order needed by each conversation.

Aggregate capacity is 20,000, apparently above demand. Yet H's partition receives 7,000 against a 5,000 ceiling, so its backlog grows 2,000 events per second. The other partitions have spare capacity that cannot automatically process the same ordered key. Adding consumers beyond the useful per-partition ownership model does not remove this bottleneck. Intermediate state is increasing lag for H while fleet-average utilization looks comfortable.

If the real invariant is per conversation, rekey H's events by conversation identity and distribute those independent sequences across suitable partitions. Preserve each conversation's order during migration, for example through a versioned routing boundary and a drain or handoff protocol. If all H's events truly require one serial decision, rekeying cannot manufacture safe parallelism; optimize the serial path, aggregate compatible operations, or renegotiate the invariant.

Failure variation: changing partition count changes a simple hash-to-partition mapping while old events remain in prior partitions. Consumers can observe old and new records concurrently unless a migration protocol preserves the intended boundary. Use a planned key-routing version, explicit sequence validation, or a supported product strategy rather than assuming partition expansion preserves every per-key application property automatically. Final outcome is stable load only if the new partitioning matches independent business units. Interview takeaway: calculate per-key and per-partition load before trusting aggregate throughput.

Read order is not completion order

A consumer can fetch ordered records and dispatch them to a parallel worker pool, then complete later records first. If updates to one entity must be applied in order, preserve serial execution for that entity, use version preconditions, or buffer out-of-order completions. The broker cannot repair an application that discards its ordering discipline after fetch. State whether the requirement concerns append order, delivery order, state-application order, or externally visible effects.

Chapter summary

Partition keys encode the scope of order and parallelism. Hot keys and downstream concurrent processing can invalidate assumptions that a fleet-wide throughput number conceals.

03 · Acknowledgments and durability

Know what survives after the producer hears success

Replication count, acknowledgment policy, and recovery rules jointly define exposure.

Durable append is a protocol boundary

A producer sends a batch, the broker writes records, replicas receive state, and an acknowledgment returns. A failure can occur between any stages. Specify whether success requires a leader copy, a set of replicas, or another durable boundary. Replication factor alone is insufficient: a cluster configured with multiple replicas can still acknowledge under a weaker condition, and an unsafe recovery policy can later select a replica missing acknowledged records.

Distinguish the maximum configured replica set from the set currently considered eligible by the protocol. A minimum-replica admission rule can prevent acknowledgments when too few safe copies remain, at the cost of write availability. That rule must align with failure-domain placement. Three replicas on one host or one shared failure surface do not provide the same protection as independent copies. Read the product's exact durability guarantees, including persistence assumptions and leader recovery behavior.

Producer idempotence can prevent certain duplicates caused by retries within a supported producer protocol. It does not identify every duplicate business event generated by two application instances or an upstream retry outside that scope. A producer transaction may atomically group broker operations under defined visibility rules; it does not automatically transact with an arbitrary payment API or external database. Keep claims scoped to the actual boundary.

Retry identity scopes

Worked example 2: acknowledgment lost after append

Initial state: business event E9 is submitted to a replicated log. The leader appends E9, obtains the required replica confirmations, then its response is lost. The producer cannot distinguish accepted from unaccepted using the timeout alone. The objective is to avoid losing E9 while controlling duplicates. It retries under the configured producer identity and sequence contract if supported, and retains business event identity E9 regardless of transport outcome.

With an applicable idempotent producer protocol, the retry can be recognized as the same producer sequence and avoid an extra append. Without that protection, the log may contain two records carrying E9. Consumers must use business deduplication where duplicate effects would be harmful. Intermediate state includes uncertainty at the producer despite durable acceptance. A timeout is not a reason to discard the event or generate a new unrelated identity.

Failure variation: the producer process loses its local state and restarts under a different identity. A guarantee tied to the previous producer session may not cover the new append as the same transport operation. The application-level event key still allows downstream reconciliation. Another variation is a leader failure before the configured durability threshold; whether the event survives depends on the actual protocol, not the producer's hope that a socket write meant success.

Final outcome is either one broker append under the supported producer contract or repeatable business processing despite duplicate append occurrences. Production implication: monitor acknowledgment latency, retries, eligible replica health, and append errors separately. An ingestion API should report whether it accepted durable responsibility for an event, rather than proxying a vague transport success. Interview takeaway: explain the uncertain-outcome window and name which identity closes which duplicate risk.

Batching changes both efficiency and failure scope

Batching amortizes request overhead and can improve compression and storage efficiency. Waiting to fill a batch adds latency, while large batches increase memory and the amount of work affected by retries or partial downstream processing. Bound batch bytes as well as record count because AI payload sizes vary widely. A batch of ten metadata events and ten large prompts have very different memory and network costs. Keep durability and latency measurements tied to the actual batch policy.

Chapter summary

Durability is defined by acknowledgment and recovery rules, not replica count alone. Producer identity can narrow transport duplicates, while business identity remains necessary at the application boundary.

04 · Consumer progress and effects

Commit progress only when its meaning is true

An offset is a durable claim about what the consumer may safely skip on restart.

The two classic crash windows

Suppose a consumer reads a record, updates a database, then records progress. A crash after the update but before progress can cause redelivery and duplicate application. If it records progress first and crashes before the update, the record may be skipped permanently. Moving the offset call does not eliminate the problem; it chooses which failure window you expose unless the effect and progress share an atomic boundary or the effect is repeat-safe.

For an external transactional database, a consumer can store the event identity and business update in one transaction. A uniqueness constraint or conditional insert prevents applying the same event twice. The progress record may then be derived or committed under a design that tolerates redelivery. If several independent consumers intentionally create different projections, scope deduplication by projection or effect type as well as event identity. A global “seen” flag could incorrectly suppress legitimate work.

For a nontransactional remote API, use its supported idempotency key or outcome lookup. If neither exists, preserve an unknown outcome and reconcile rather than promising exactly once. Broker transactions can be valuable within supported consume-transform-produce workflows, but an external system must cooperate for its effects to share that guarantee. This is the same cross-service boundary you encounter when orchestrating cloud recovery actions.

Consumer crash windows
OrderCrash riskRequired protection
Progress before effectSkipped workAtomicity / different responsibility contract
Effect before progressRepeated effectAtomic marker or repeat-safe provider
Parallel later completionSkipping earlier gapContiguous safe frontier

Worked example 3: effect succeeds before progress commit

Initial state: consumer C is processing partition P at record 100. The next restart position is currently 100, meaning record 100 has not been safely acknowledged under this example's convention. The event requests an index update with logical identity E100. C writes the projection and a deduplication row for E100 in one database transaction, then crashes before advancing transport progress to 101. The objective is one accepted projection update without losing replayability.

After restart, C reads record 100 again. Its database transaction sees the existing E100 marker and returns the already-applied outcome without applying the update twice. It then safely advances progress to 101 under its progress protocol. Intermediate state has a completed effect and stale transport progress; repeat processing is expected. Final outcome is one projection effect and a correct restart frontier.

Failure variation: C writes the marker in one transaction and the projection in another. A crash between them can make redelivery skip an update that never happened. The marker and effect must share the intended atomic boundary. Another variation is a remote tool that accepts E100 but loses its response. A local marker saying “dispatched” cannot prove completion, so the recovery state remains unknown until provider evidence resolves it.

Production implication: retain deduplication records for the supported replay horizon, not just the happy-path retry interval. If operators replay a month of history after markers were deleted, the system can repeat effects it previously suppressed. Define whether replays rebuild a new projection namespace or revisit the same effect namespace. Interview takeaway: progress is safe when repeated processing is safe or when progress and effects commit atomically under a real contract.

Parallel processing needs a contiguous frontier

If records 100, 101, and 102 run concurrently and only 102 completes, committing restart position 103 skips unfinished work. Track completed records and advance only through the contiguous completed prefix under your ordering policy. Bound the gap buffer so one slow record cannot create unlimited memory. If the application allows independent keys to progress, maintain a more appropriate per-key or task acknowledgment model rather than claiming one scalar offset represents arbitrary out-of-order completion.

Chapter summary

Recorded progress must mean that skipped work is safely complete under replay. Atomic effect markers, provider contracts, and contiguous completion frontiers solve different parts of that problem.

05 · Ownership changes and replay

A consumer rebalance is a recovery protocol

Partition assignment does not instantly stop old application work.

Consumer groups and stale executors

A consumer group divides partitions among members under its assignment protocol. Joining, leaving, failure suspicion, and configuration changes can move ownership. The broker or coordinator can establish which consumer may fetch or commit progress under a group generation, but application work already dispatched to an external pool may continue after assignment changes. A stale consumer can therefore still create an external effect unless the effect boundary checks its authority or supports safe deduplication.

On revocation, stop launching new work for the affected partition, drain or cancel under a defined deadline, record only safe progress, and preserve unresolved attempts for recovery. Exact client hooks and supported cooperative behavior are version-sensitive. Design the application contract first, then map it to the selected client protocol. Do not assume a callback makes every remote operation cease synchronously.

A long-running GPU task can exceed the consumer's coordination expectations even though it is doing useful work. Separate record discovery from durable task execution if jobs naturally outlive consumer sessions. The consumer can transactionally create or discover a durable task and advance its own progress once responsibility is safely transferred. A task scheduler then owns leases, retries, and outputs. This avoids keeping a broker partition hostage to a single hours-long computation while preserving a clear responsibility boundary.

Rebalance recovery
ObservationSafe response

Long task still runningAdopt durable task identity

Worked example 4: old consumer returns after reassignment

Initial state: C1 owns partition P under group generation 12 and has dispatched event E200 to an external indexer. C1 stops communicating with the group coordinator. P moves to C2 under generation 13, which restarts from the last safe recorded position. The objective is correct effects despite overlap. C2 rediscovers E200 and consults the projection's durable event marker or task identity instead of assuming C1 never acted.

If E200 already committed, C2 adopts the result and advances safe progress. If a durable task exists but is still uncertain, C2 transfers discovery responsibility without creating another unrelated task. C1 returns and tries to commit progress under the stale group generation; the supported group protocol should reject stale authority for that operation. That protection does not retroactively cancel C1's external indexer request.

Failure variation: E200 represents a destructive cloud action and the provider does not check the consumer generation. Use stable business effect identity and provider reconciliation, or enforce a separate execution authority before dispatch. Relying on group membership alone is insufficient. Intermediate state can legitimately include two consumers aware of the same event, while the business protocol ensures one accepted effect under its own boundary.

Final outcome is a correct projection or durable task transfer, with progress committed only after responsibility is known. Production implication: observe rebalance frequency, revoked-but-running work, duplicate discoveries, and uncertain effects. Excessively aggressive coordination timeouts can cause churn and amplify load, while very slow detection delays reassignment. Interview takeaway: consumer coordination protects transport ownership; application effect ownership must be designed explicitly.

Replay is a product operation

Operators replay to rebuild an index, repair a bug, validate a new model, or recover lost state. Each purpose needs a declared effect namespace, schema compatibility, authorization, and resource budget. A rebuild into a fresh index can intentionally reprocess every event without repeating external emails or cloud actions. Route replay traffic separately from live work and prevent a catch-up job from consuming the entire GPU or database budget. Replaying history is not automatically safe just because records are immutable.

Chapter summary

Assignment changes create overlapping observations and uncertain work. Separate transport progress, durable task ownership, and external effect safety, and treat replay as an authorized workload with explicit semantics.

06 · Backpressure and overload

Bound work before the cheapest queue becomes an expensive failure

Pressure must propagate across every buffering layer.

Backpressure versus retrying harder

Backpressure means a downstream capacity constraint changes upstream production or admission. It can use bounded queues, credits, explicit rejection, demand-driven pulls, concurrency limits, or rate budgets. A consumer that keeps fetching while its GPU executor is saturated has merely moved the backlog into memory. A producer that retries immediately after rejection increases offered load. Trace all buffers: client, gateway, producer, broker, consumer fetch, executor, database pool, and provider. Every unbounded stage can hide the real bottleneck until it fails.

Use both count and byte bounds. A queue of one thousand inference requests can be small or enormous depending on prompt length, output reservation, and attached data. For execution, a concurrency budget can protect memory even when request rate is modest. For ingestion, a byte-rate budget can protect storage and replication. For external tools, a provider-specific request budget may dominate. One requests-per-second limit cannot represent every resource constraint.

Bounded admission creates an explicit response when capacity is exhausted. Options include reject with retriable guidance, defer into a durable asynchronous contract, drop low-value telemetry under policy, or shed optional work. Choose by business semantics. You cannot silently drop accepted recovery commands, while some sampled metrics can be intentionally lossy. Persisted acceptance should imply a responsibility the system can actually honor within retention and deadline constraints.

Worked example 5: a burst fills every hidden buffer

Initial state: arrivals jump to 1,000 tasks per second while execution sustains 600. The durable queue currently holds 20,000 tasks and has a policy maximum of 80,000 under the stated task-size and retention model. The objective is to preserve accepted work and prevent uncontrolled latency or memory growth. Net accumulation is 400 tasks per second, so the remaining 60,000 slots fill in 150 seconds if rates stay fixed.

The consumer's local executor queue is bounded at 1,200 tasks. It must stop or reduce fetching when those slots are occupied, retaining coordination heartbeats as required by its client. Upstream admission should react before the durable queue reaches its hard limit, using a queue-age or budget threshold. If the maximum useful deadline is thirty seconds, allowing 80,000 queued tasks at 600 completions per second implies a much larger nominal drain delay, so the count limit is not an adequate latency policy.

Suppose demand returns to 400 tasks per second after ninety seconds. During the burst, backlog grew by 36,000 to 56,000. With 600 service and 400 ongoing arrivals, the net drain is 200 tasks per second. Clearing that backlog to zero takes 280 seconds under the fixed-rate model, not 56,000 divided by 600. Intermediate state continues processing new arrivals while catching up old work. Admission policy may target a lower steady queue rather than absolute zero.

Failure variation: clients retry rejected work with no jitter, so effective arrival rate stays above capacity even after the original burst ends. Publish bounded retry guidance, enforce per-tenant budgets, and distinguish original requests from attempts. Final outcome is controlled durable buffering and a visible admission response, not a claim that every offered request met its deadline. Interview takeaway: calculate fill time and net catch-up time, then make pressure visible before memory or retention is exhausted.

Credits and cancellation release real capacity

A credit represents permission to have a bounded amount of work in flight. Return it only when the corresponding resource is actually released, not when a request times out at the client. A canceled inference may still run unless the execution layer observes cancellation. If clients abandon requests but compute continues indefinitely, admission can overestimate available capacity. Track accepted, queued, running, canceled-requested, and terminated states separately where resource accounting depends on them.

Chapter summary

Backpressure is an end-to-end resource contract. Bounded buffers, admission, concurrency, retry policy, deadlines, and real resource release must agree across the pipeline.

07 · Retention, storage, and recovery windows

Retention determines whether replay is still possible

Durable storage is finite, and consumer progress does not necessarily control deletion.

Time, bytes, compaction, and usable history

A retained log needs a deletion policy. Time-based retention, byte-based limits, and key-based compaction serve different purposes. Compaction can preserve a recent value per key under its actual implementation contract, but it is not a universal substitute for a complete event history. If a consumer rebuild needs every transition, retaining only a latest value can erase required information. Tombstones, deletion timing, and lagging readers need particular care.

Do not assume a broker retains records until every consumer has processed them. Many retained-log designs delete according to configured policies independent of a slow consumer's progress. That makes consumer recovery bounded by the retained window. A slow consumer may need a snapshot plus a compatible replay frontier rather than reading from an ancient offset. The snapshot must correspond to a defined log boundary so no changes are lost or applied inconsistently.

AI data adds privacy and payload-lifecycle concerns. A deletion request may require removing source documents, index entries, cached embeddings, and retained event payloads under policy. Keeping a hashed reference does not automatically make sensitive metadata harmless. Coordinate retention and replay with governance, and avoid claiming that immutable means legally or operationally undeletable. Encryption-key destruction can be part of a policy only when its actual coverage and consequences are understood.

Worked example 6: size the replay window

Initial state: an event stream produces 50,000 records per second, averaging 800 stored bytes per record before replication and additional overhead. The desired replay window is 24 hours, and there are three replicated copies. The objective is a first-order storage estimate with explicit units. Logical data rate is 40,000,000 bytes per second, or 40 MB per second in decimal units. One day contains 86,400 seconds, yielding 3.456 TB of logical record data.

Three copies require 10.368 TB before indexes, segment slack, metadata, compression effects, headroom, and operational reserve. The estimate is not a disk purchase recommendation. Measure the actual stored size and replication behavior for the chosen configuration. If the broker pool can safely devote only 8 TB to this stream, the nominal 24-hour promise is unsupported at these assumptions. A byte limit may remove history earlier than the time target.

Intermediate state: a consumer is down for twenty hours while the stream continues. It needs enough catch-up throughput to avoid the oldest required records expiring before it reads them. Retention equal to expected outage duration provides no catch-up margin. A valid recovery calculation includes downtime, detection, repair, and net drain under ongoing production. You may increase retention, reduce ingestion, provision more consumption, or restore from a compatible snapshot.

Failure variation: average stored record size doubles because events now embed prompts. The logical daily data doubles, and consumer network or parsing capacity may also fall. Update admission, storage reserve, and privacy analysis together. Final outcome is a bounded replay contract supported by measured storage and recovery headroom. Interview takeaway: retention is a capacity and recovery property, not a label that guarantees infinite history.

Separate poison records from ordinary lag

A malformed event can repeatedly fail one ordered partition and block valid later work. Validate schemas at ingestion, classify permanent errors, and define a quarantine or dead-letter policy with durable reason and identity. Skipping a record may violate state invariants, so do not automatically route every failure away to keep lag green. Some events require stopping the affected entity until repair. Quarantine must preserve access controls and retention, and replaying repaired records needs an explicit ordering and deduplication procedure.

Chapter summary

Retention must cover outage and catch-up under realistic bytes, replication, and overhead. Snapshots, compaction, quarantine, and deletion policy each change what a replay can correctly reconstruct.

08 · AI pipelines and operational context

Control pressure at model and tool boundaries

Broker health does not establish useful AI throughput or safe business completion.

Embedding and retrieval freshness

An ingestion log can feed chunking, embedding, indexing, access-policy updates, and audit. Each stage has its own progress frontier. A source event acknowledged by the broker is not yet searchable. Define freshness from source acceptance to the version visible in authorized query results. Record document version, chunking strategy, embedding model, and index publication so replay does not silently mix incompatible representations.

A new embedding model can require rebuilding into a separate index namespace. Replay traffic should have its own budgets and should not starve live updates. If an access revocation arrives behind a large rebuild backlog, security may require a separate policy path or query-time enforcement. Do not let data-processing convenience turn a slow optional recomputation into a delayed authorization decision. The relevant order may differ for document content and policy.

Meta's historical LogDevice publication provides public context for durable ordered logs serving data-intensive systems. Google's MillWheel publication supplies stream-processing reliability context. Neither source establishes the invented embedding pipeline, its throughput, or a private deployment at the five target companies. The lesson uses these sources as grounding while keeping the production design hypothetical.

Inference batching and execution pressure

Inference systems often queue briefly to form batches, but waiting improves efficiency only while it respects latency and memory limits. NVIDIA's Triton documentation describes dynamic-batching queue policies and execution resource limiting. These are useful examples of separate queue and execution controls, not a guarantee that one server setting provides global tenant fairness. A platform still needs admission, per-tenant budgets, and cancellation propagation across gateways and model instances.

Measure useful token or request completion under the declared prompt and output distributions. An arrival-rate limit alone can admit a few huge requests that exhaust memory. A queue-length metric alone can hide many seconds of long decode work. Track bytes or tokens where appropriate, estimate remaining work carefully, and test overload with realistic length skew. Avoid translating an average benchmark into a hard per-request deadline guarantee.

Batching can also change failure scope. A poisoned input must not corrupt another tenant's response or cause unsafe shared-buffer reuse. A canceled request may need removal from a pending batch or a defined behavior once execution starts. Keep tenant identity and response correlation explicit. Correct routing of generated outputs is a security invariant, not only an application convenience.

Agents and recovery commands

Anthropic's public multi-agent account establishes a production context involving coordination and tools, not a private queue protocol. A hypothetical agent platform can use a log for durable observations and a task queue for eligible model or tool work. Pending approvals, execution ownership, and effect keys remain authoritative elsewhere or within a carefully defined transaction boundary. Replaying a conversation history must not resend every email or recovery command.

In an OCI-style DR platform, a state-change event can notify the next plan step while the durable execution record verifies its prerequisites and current epoch. During an outage, event bursts and retries should not saturate the very control plane needed to recover. Reserve budgets for authoritative actions and reconciliation, and sample or defer lower-value observations. OpenAI's public infrastructure and database accounts provide general context for burst management and overload, not evidence of this hypothetical DR implementation.

Chapter summary

Measure end-to-end freshness, inference readiness, and effect completion separately from broker delivery. Apply pressure controls to the actual scarce resource and preserve authorization through replay.

09 · Production pipeline architecture

Design a replayable enterprise AI event platform

Protect durable acceptance, tenant order, controlled execution, and observable business freshness.

Requirements, interfaces, and records

Design a hypothetical platform ingesting 50,000 document and workflow events per second, with a 24-hour replay objective, independent audit and indexing consumers, and bursty GPU embedding. Assume entity-local order is sufficient for document updates, while destructive workflow steps require separate execution authority. Tenant data residency and authorization are mandatory. Live updates should not be starved by replay, and accepted work must either complete under policy or reach a durable explicit failure state.

The ingestion API accepts tenant-scoped event identity, entity key, schema version, causation metadata, and a controlled payload reference. It validates policy and size, then records durable responsibility through the chosen database/outbox or direct append contract. Return an acceptance ID and status reference rather than claiming immediate searchability. For state-changing commands, distinguish requested action from observed event so consumers cannot confuse an unapproved instruction with evidence that the action happened.

A partitioned retained log supplies independent consumer progress. An indexing coordinator converts events into durable tasks, deduplicated by projection namespace and event identity. GPU executors have bounded concurrency and byte or token budgets. An index publisher verifies complete versioned output before making it queryable. An audit consumer has its own access policy and progress. A workflow coordinator validates current plan revision, approval, and ownership before dispatching effectful steps; a queue notification alone cannot authorize them.

Persist event identity and schema, partition-routing version, payload hash and lifecycle, accepted task identity, per-projection effect marker, output version, failure reason, and progress frontier. Store enough evidence to answer whether an event was accepted, appended, converted to a task, executed, published, or quarantined. This chain allows precise customer status and operational diagnosis. A single “processed” boolean cannot represent every stage safely.

Normal flow, replay, and failures

Normal flow validates and appends, assigns an entity-consistent partition, discovers records, creates repeat-safe tasks, runs bounded execution, and publishes accepted output. The consumer advances its own progress after durable responsibility transfer or completed projection according to its declared protocol. Live and replay traffic use separate budgets and effect namespaces when appropriate. A rebuild writes to a new index version, then compares quality and access checks before switching the query alias.

If a producer loses an append response, it retries using the supported transport identity while retaining the business event identity. If a consumer crashes after an effect, redelivery consults the transactional marker or provider outcome. If a rebalance happens during a long GPU task, the durable task owns execution independently of the consumer session. If retention expires before a consumer catches up, restore a compatible snapshot and replay from its frontier instead of pretending a missing prefix never mattered.

A malformed event is quarantined only under a policy that preserves the entity's consistency needs. A bad content update may allow a documented prior version to remain visible; an authorization revocation may require blocking access until policy is repaired. Define these failure responses by operation. Retry transient infrastructure failures with budgets and jitter, but do not endlessly retry a permanent schema error. Keep repair and replay actions audited and tenant scoped.

Capacity, isolation, observability, and evolution

The first-order 40 MB per second logical rate and three-copy 10.368 TB daily estimate are starting points. Benchmark actual record sizes, compression, replication traffic, consumer fan-out, and storage overhead. If three independent consumers read the full stream, outbound read volume can be materially different from append volume. Partition count should reflect throughput, recovery parallelism, key skew, and operational overhead, not only the maximum number of consumers someone hopes to launch.

Enforce tenant authentication at ingestion and authorization at payload access and query. A consumer granted broad topic access must not become an uncontrolled path around document-level policy. Encrypt transport and storage, isolate sensitive streams where needed, and test deletion and retention behavior. Dead-letter storage often receives the worst-formed and most sensitive payloads; give it the same governance attention as the primary pipeline.

Observe accepted-to-visible freshness, partition lag in time and bytes, hot-key concentration, contiguous completion gaps, retry amplification, queue age, memory pressure, task uncertainty, quarantine reasons, and retention headroom. Page on customer freshness or impending unrecoverable lag rather than only broker process health. Runbooks include pausing replay, throttling producers, isolating a hot tenant, increasing eligible consumption, repairing a poison event, and restoring from snapshot.

Start with a small set of clear schemas and a simple partition strategy. Evolve toward dedicated hot-tenant routing or separate workload classes when measured skew and isolation justify it. Migrate schemas compatibly: consumers may lag behind producers, so a deployment rollback does not remove already-published new-version records. Preserve old readers or an explicit transformation strategy through the replay horizon. Defend the architecture by its recovery evidence and business observations, not by the popularity of the broker.

Chapter summary

A replayable AI event platform joins durable acceptance, entity order, repeat-safe tasks, bounded execution, governed payloads, and versioned publication. Each stage needs a status and recovery contract.

10 · Executable correctness and capacity models

Advance only through safe completed work

Model contiguous progress and net backlog movement with explicit conventions.

A completion frontier and queue calculation

The executable Python model tracks a next-unprocessed position and a set of completed record numbers. Completing a later record adds it to the set but does not advance past an unfinished gap. When the missing prefix completes, the frontier advances through every contiguous completed position. This models one scalar restart frontier under an application contract where those integer positions are contiguous. Some real log abstractions have gaps or different visibility semantics, so adapt the model to their actual next-record API rather than assuming all products use this numbering.

The model also calculates bounded-queue fill time and net drain. Incoming work continues during catch-up, so service minus input is the useful drain rate. If input is at least service and backlog is positive, the fixed-rate model cannot drain. These functions are arithmetic teaching tools, not broker clients or concurrency-safe production code. Their assumptions are stated so a reviewer can reject a misleading use.

PYTHON · executable standard-library model
from math import inf

class Frontier:
    # Teaching convention: next_position is the next unfinished contiguous integer.
    def __init__(self, next_position):
        self.next_position = next_position
        self.completed = set()

    def complete(self, position):
        if position < self.next_position:
            return self.next_position
        self.completed.add(position)
        while self.next_position in self.completed:
            self.completed.remove(self.next_position)
            self.next_position += 1
        return self.next_position

def fill_time(current, maximum, incoming, service):
    if min(current, maximum, incoming, service) < 0 or current > maximum:
        raise ValueError('invalid queue model')
    if current == maximum:
        return 0.0
    return (maximum - current) / (incoming - service) if incoming > service else inf

def drain_time(backlog, incoming, service):
    if min(backlog, incoming, service) < 0:
        raise ValueError('negative input')
    if backlog == 0:
        return 0.0
    return backlog / (service - incoming) if service > incoming else inf

f = Frontier(100)
assert f.complete(102) == 100
assert f.complete(100) == 101
assert f.complete(101) == 103
assert f.complete(100) == 103
assert fill_time(20_000, 80_000, 1000, 600) == 150
assert drain_time(56_000, 400, 600) == 280
assert 50_000 * 800 * 86_400 * 3 == 10_368_000_000_000
print('safe next position:', f.next_position)
print('fill: 150 s; drain: 280 s; three-copy record data: 10.368 TB')

The assertions show record 102 finishing before 100 and 101 without prematurely committing restart position 103. They reproduce the 150-second burst-fill estimate, 280-second post-burst drain, and 10.368 TB three-copy daily record-data estimate. The arithmetic uses decimal bytes and fixed rates. A production plan adds distributions, overhead, storage reserve, and measurement uncertainty. The frontier's loop does work proportional to the number of newly contiguous completed records; each inserted position is removed at most once, while memory can grow with the out-of-order gap unless bounded.

For an integration test, place the effect and event marker in a real transactional database, crash after commit but before broker progress, and verify that replay returns the same logical outcome. Then break the atomicity by separating marker and effect transactions and observe the counterexample. This is a useful exercise because it turns an abstract delivery slogan into a concrete database and crash trace.

11 · Original interview practice

Defend the boundary behind every delivery guarantee

Original Senior Staff questions, not verified company interview reports.

Twelve queue and log questions

1. When would you choose a retained log over a work queue?

Evaluation: workload semantics. Use a retained log when independent consumers need their own progress and replayable ordered history. Use a work-distribution contract when competing workers execute tasks under acknowledgment or lease semantics. Products can combine features, so inspect actual behavior. Follow-up: do completed tasks need rebuilding later? Common mistake: choosing by product popularity. Tradeoff: replay adds retention, schema, governance, and consumer-recovery responsibilities.

2. Why is total broker capacity insufficient for a hot tenant?

Evaluation: partition constraints. A key requiring one ordered path can exceed one partition's usable capacity despite spare aggregate throughput. Determine the real invariant and split independent entities only if semantics permit. Follow-up: partition count changes. Preserve the ordering boundary during rekeying. Common mistake: adding consumers without changing the bottleneck. Tradeoff: finer keys improve parallelism while complicating cross-key invariants.

3. What does a producer timeout tell you?

Evaluation: uncertainty and identity. The append may have succeeded. Retry under the applicable producer protocol and retain stable business event identity. Session-specific transport idempotence may not cover a new producer incarnation or duplicate upstream generation. Follow-up: the process restarts without its old identity. Explain downstream deduplication. Common mistake: treating timeout as definite rejection. Tradeoff: stronger durability and retry contracts add state and may reduce availability.

4. Where should the consumer commit its offset?

Evaluation: effect boundary. There is no universal line of code that removes both crash windows. Commit progress only after safe responsibility transfer or effect completion under replay-safe or atomic semantics. For a database, marker and update can share a transaction. Follow-up: arbitrary external API. Require cooperation or reconciliation. Common mistake: claiming after-effect commit alone prevents duplicates. Tradeoff: replay-safe processing accepts repeated work to avoid loss.

5. Does an exactly-once broker feature make a payment exactly once?

Evaluation: scope discipline. Only if the payment effect participates in a supported contract establishing that outcome. Broker transactions do not automatically include an arbitrary provider. Use stable action identity, provider deduplication, or lookup. Follow-up: provider retention expires. Preserve uncertainty and reconcile. Common mistake: extending transport guarantees to business effects. Tradeoff: pausing ambiguous effects protects correctness while delaying completion.

6. Can you commit position 103 when only record 102 completed?

Evaluation: progress frontiers. Not if 100 and 101 remain unprocessed and restart at 103 would skip them. Track the contiguous safe prefix or use a different acknowledgment model suited to independent tasks. Follow-up: one gap stalls memory. Bound in-flight work and diagnose the blocked record. Common mistake: recording the highest completed number. Tradeoff: parallel processing improves throughput but adds ordering and gap management.

7. A rebalance moved the partition. Can the old worker still act?

Evaluation: application ownership. Already-dispatched work may continue. Group-generation checks can protect transport progress under the protocol but do not necessarily fence external effects. Transfer durable task responsibility or deduplicate effects. Follow-up: a long GPU task exceeds coordination timing. Separate discovery from execution. Common mistake: assuming revocation synchronously kills all work. Tradeoff: durable tasks add infrastructure but fit long-running execution.

8. How long until the burst fills the queue?

Evaluation: net-rate modeling. At 1,000 arrivals and 600 service per second, 60,000 remaining slots fill in 150 seconds under fixed rates. Include byte limits and deadline usefulness. Follow-up: input drops to 400 with 56,000 queued. Net drain is 200, giving 280 seconds. Common mistake: dividing by full service while arrivals continue. Tradeoff: buffering absorbs finite bursts but increases latency and retention exposure.

9. Why can consumer backpressure still fail to protect the pipeline?

Evaluation: end-to-end buffering. Fetch may stop while upstream producer buffers, gateway queues, or client retries grow unbounded. Trace count, byte, concurrency, and deadline budgets at every stage. Follow-up: client cancellation leaves compute running. Return credits only on real release. Common mistake: solving pressure in one process only. Tradeoff: explicit rejection preserves resources but requires a clear client retry contract.

10. Is 24-hour retention enough for a 24-hour consumer outage?

Evaluation: recoverability. Not necessarily: repair and net catch-up require margin, and byte limits may trim earlier. Size stored bytes, replicas, overhead, and drain rate. Consider snapshot-plus-log recovery. Follow-up: payload size doubles. Recompute storage and processing. Common mistake: treating a time setting as guaranteed usable history. Tradeoff: longer retention costs storage and may increase privacy obligations.

11. Should every poison event go to a dead-letter queue?

Evaluation: business invariants. Skipping may be safe for some telemetry but unsafe for ordered state or permission revocation. Classify the event, preserve identity and reason, and define repair/replay. Quarantine remains governed sensitive storage. Follow-up: later updates depend on the failed one. Pause the entity if needed. Common mistake: optimizing lag by silently dropping required state. Tradeoff: containment improves progress but must preserve meaning.

12. How do you replay a new index without repeating cloud actions?

Evaluation: effect namespaces and access. Use a new projection namespace, explicit replay authorization and budgets, and a pipeline that does not interpret historical observations as fresh action commands. Validate schema, payload availability, and policy. Follow-up: old dedup markers expired. Define which effects can legally repeat. Common mistake: assuming immutable history makes replay harmless. Tradeoff: separate replay paths cost resources but protect live traffic and business safety.

Architecture defense: retention is expiring during a model rebuild

The interviewer pauses the GPU fleet, doubles document size, and demands no lost updates while a new embedding index rebuilds. Clarify accepted responsibility, freshness deadlines, live versus replay priority, and whether snapshots exist. A strong design throttles or rejects before unrecoverable retention risk, isolates live policy updates, reserves catch-up capacity, and builds a new versioned projection. It calculates the new byte and net-drain envelope, records explicit degradation, and does not claim that a bigger in-memory queue solves the problem. If the constraints are infeasible, present concrete capacity or contract changes.

Chapter summary

Strong answers name ordering scope, acknowledgment semantics, effect boundaries, and capacity assumptions. Delivery slogans become useful only when you can trace the corresponding failure window.

12 · Speaking and recall

Explain asynchronous systems without vague guarantees

Make acceptance, order, replay, and business completion legible.

Thirty-second explanation

A queue or retained log separates acceptance from processing, but it does not remove capacity limits. I define durability, ordering scope, consumer progress, replay, and business effects independently. Stable identities make retries explainable. Bounded buffers and admission propagate pressure. Correct recovery requires safe effects and a progress frontier that cannot skip unfinished work.

Two-minute explanation

I would first ask whether this is competing task execution or several independent consumers of retained history. Then I would choose the ordering key from the business invariant. A conversation or document may need a serial sequence, while unrelated entities can progress in parallel. Aggregate broker throughput does not solve a hot ordered key.

At ingestion, I would specify when durable responsibility is accepted and retain a stable business event identity. Producer retries can be uncertain even after an append succeeds. At consumption, I would define what recorded progress permits us to skip on restart. An effect followed by a crash can repeat, so I need an atomic marker and update, provider deduplication, or reconciliation. Broker transactions do not automatically cover arbitrary tools.

I would bound every queue in count, bytes, and relevant execution concurrency, then propagate downstream pressure to admission. Retention must cover outage and catch-up while new events continue. For AI pipelines, I would measure source-to-searchable freshness and authorization separately from broker lag. Replays would use explicit namespaces and budgets so rebuilding an index does not resend destructive actions or starve live work. Finally, I would validate the design by crashing every acknowledgment boundary and injecting skew, poison records, and retention pressure.

Five-minute explanation

Suppose we are designing an enterprise AI event pipeline. Uploads, workflow observations, and policy updates arrive faster than downstream GPU work can always run. An asynchronous boundary is useful, but I would not begin with a broker name. I would define accepted responsibility, completion status, required order, replay horizon, and which events may cause irreversible effects.

I would partition by the smallest entity whose invariant needs order. One tenant key can become a bottleneck even when the cluster has spare capacity. If conversations are independent, they can use separate keys; if all updates require one serial authority, I cannot safely split them just to improve a benchmark. I would also preserve order through the application worker pool, because fetching records in order does not mean parallel effects finish in order.

For durable ingestion, I would choose a verified acknowledgment policy and failure-domain placement. A producer timeout leaves append outcome uncertain. Supported producer idempotence can narrow retry duplicates, while stable business identity covers a broader logical event. I would not equate replica count with guaranteed survival or extend a producer-session guarantee across arbitrary upstream retries.

Consumers need a precise progress contract. If a database update and event marker commit atomically, redelivery can return the prior outcome. If the effect is external, its provider must cooperate through idempotency or lookup, otherwise the system must reconcile uncertainty. With parallel processing, I would advance only a safe contiguous frontier under the chosen model. Recording the highest completed record can skip earlier unfinished work after a crash.

Consumer membership changes add another boundary. A revoked consumer may still have a GPU task or tool call in flight. Group ownership can protect supported transport operations, but it does not automatically cancel external work. Long-running execution can move into a durable task system so discovery progress and task ownership have separate lifecycles. Replaying an index would use a new projection namespace and would never implicitly replay every historical business action.

For overload, I would trace all buffers and scarce resources. Count limits alone are insufficient when prompts vary in size, and rate limits alone do not bound long-running concurrency. At a burst of one thousand arrivals against six hundred service per second, backlog grows four hundred per second. I would calculate fill time, enforce admission before retention or memory risk, and use net service minus continuing input for catch-up. Client retries and cancellation must participate in the same budget.

Finally, I would size the replay window from actual stored bytes, replication, overhead, outage, and drain time. A poison record needs an invariant-aware repair policy, not automatic skipping to keep lag green. I would measure business freshness and accepted completion, test authorization through replay, and roll out schemas with old consumers in mind. The architecture is credible when every success signal names the boundary it proves and every failure window has a safe recovery action.

Important concepts and final cheat sheet

  • Acceptance: durable responsibility, not final completion.
  • Partition: a defined scope of ordered history.
  • Business identity: logical event or effect across retries.
  • Transport position: one append occurrence.
  • Acknowledgment: evidence of a specified durability boundary.
  • Progress frontier: the safely skippable prefix.
  • Atomic marker: deduplication committed with its effect.
  • Rebalance: transport ownership transition.
  • Backpressure: downstream constraints changing upstream admission.
  • Credits: bounded in-flight resource permission.
  • Retention: finite usable replay history.
  • Quarantine: governed failed-event isolation.
  • Replay namespace: scope defining repeat-processing effects.
  • Freshness: source-to-usable-result delay.

Common mistakes are saying “exactly once” without a boundary, assuming DNS or consumer ownership stops external work, using fleet averages for hot partitions, and equating low lag with correct processing. Replace each shortcut with a concrete event identity, state predicate, and crash trace. Use phrases such as “the effect is outside this transaction” and “this progress record permits restart after this prefix” to make the guarantee precise.

Whiteboard and speaking practice

In thirty seconds, distinguish broker acceptance from business completion. In two minutes, trace a crash after a database effect but before progress commit. In five minutes, defend the pipeline while GPU service pauses and retention approaches its limit. On a whiteboard, label every buffer, identity, and acknowledgment. Inject one duplicate append, one stale consumer, and one out-of-order completion. Explain what can repeat, what may be skipped, and which invariant prevents loss or duplicate effects.

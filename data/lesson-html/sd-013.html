sd-013 · Scheduling foundations

A scheduler makes promises about scarce resources

Separate admission, placement, execution, and durable completion before optimizing utilization.

Start with useful work, not a queue consumer

A team builds a distributed job system by putting messages on a queue and launching a worker for each message. The prototype is easy to understand. Production adds GPU memory limits, tenant quotas, multiple tasks that must start together, workers that disappear, and actions that must not execute twice. A queue answers which items are waiting. A scheduler must decide which work may run, where it fits, when resources are exclusively reserved, and what happens when observations are stale.

Define a job as the user's logical request, a task as a schedulable unit within that request, and an attempt as one execution incarnation of a task. A training job may have many cooperating tasks, each with multiple attempts over its lifetime. A batch embedding request may have thousands of independent shards. An agent workflow may schedule model calls and tool calls with different security and retry requirements. Mixing logical identity and process identity makes recovery ambiguous.

Admission determines whether the platform accepts a job under quota, policy, and feasibility constraints. Queueing determines when accepted work becomes eligible. Placement chooses a resource assignment. Reservation commits that assignment against concurrent competitors. Dispatch asks a worker to execute it. Reconciliation compares desired and observed state. Completion publication records an accepted logical outcome. These stages can be implemented in fewer services, but their correctness boundaries should remain explicit.

The scheduler's safety invariant might say that committed GPU allocations never exceed a node's allocatable capacity, and only a current task attempt may publish its output. Its liveness objective might say that an eligible admitted job eventually starts under a stable capacity and fairness model. Neither property follows merely from delivering a message at least once. External business effects add another invariant that may require provider-side identity or a single effect owner.

Understand workloads before selecting policies

Interactive inference values latency and warm capacity. Batch indexing values throughput and can often retry a shard. Large training needs coordinated resource shapes and expensive state recovery. Recovery orchestration may be urgent but low volume and effectful. A single FIFO queue for all four workloads creates hidden interference. Explicit classes let you reserve latency-sensitive capacity, backfill independent tasks, and prioritize disaster recovery without granting every customer an unrestricted urgent flag.

Your OCI control-plane and Full Stack Disaster Recovery background maps naturally to this design. A desired recovery step is distinct from the worker process carrying it out. Durable step identity, ownership epochs, preconditions, and reconciliation are familiar ideas that become especially important when AI tasks consume costly accelerators. The example scheduler in this lesson is hypothetical; it is not a claim about OCI internals or a project you personally implemented.

How I would explain this in an interview: “I separate accepting work from promising a particular placement. The scheduler operates on durable logical jobs and resource reservations; workers execute fenced attempts. Queue delivery can repeat, so completion and external effects require their own identity contracts. I optimize utilization only after those boundaries are safe.” Pause and explain why a running container is not sufficient evidence that a logical task completed.

Chapter summary

A production scheduler connects intent, policy, resources, and outcomes. Logical jobs, task attempts, queue messages, and worker processes are different identities with different lifecycles.

02 · Durable state and work discovery

Make eligibility recoverable after every crash

The queue is an index of work, not the only place the business request exists.

Define the persistent state machine

A task can move from admitted to waiting, ready, reserved, starting, running, and succeeded, with explicit retryable, permanently failed, canceling, and canceled outcomes. Do not overload one failed flag with every kind of uncertainty. A worker timeout does not establish that execution stopped. A dispatch timeout does not establish that the worker never received the command. Use states that preserve what is known so a successor can reconcile rather than guess.

Persist immutable job specification, tenant, creation identity, dependency edges, resource requirements, execution policy, and specification revision. Mutable task state includes state revision, active attempt ID, reservation reference, owner epoch, retry eligibility time, cancellation generation, and output reference. Use conditional updates to prevent two controllers from advancing the same state based on different stale observations. Append audit events when useful, but choose one authoritative state representation and define how indexes derive from it.

Submission should be idempotent under a tenant-scoped request key. Atomically persist the job and an outbox record or another durable discovery mechanism before acknowledging. A dispatcher publishes the outbox record to the work queue and marks it processed under a repeat-safe protocol. If the queue notification is lost or duplicated, reconciliation can rediscover an eligible task from the authoritative store. The outbox bridges discovery; it does not make every downstream effect atomic.

Worked example 1: the accepted job with a lost notification

Initial state: tenant T submits embedding job J with request key K1. The database transaction creates J, tasks T1 through T100, and durable outbox event O1. The objective is to ensure that an acknowledged job cannot vanish because a queue publication failed. The API returns J only after the transaction commits. The dispatcher reads O1, publishes a discovery message, then crashes before recording that publication succeeded.

On restart, O1 is still pending, so the dispatcher publishes again. Two consumers may now discover J. Each reads authoritative task state and tries to reserve a ready task with the expected revision. Only one conditional transition for a particular task succeeds. The loser refreshes rather than launches an unreserved duplicate. Intermediate state may contain duplicate notifications, but it contains one committed active attempt per task under the stated transaction boundary.

Final outcome: all eligible tasks remain discoverable, and duplicate notifications do not authorize duplicate reservations. Failure variation: the database committed but the client lost the response and resubmits K1. The API returns the original J if the canonical request matches, and rejects reuse of K1 with incompatible parameters. Generating a new job because the client did not see the first response would defeat logical idempotency.

Another variation is an outbox cleanup bug that deletes O1 before publication. A periodic scan of ready state can provide a recovery path, but its latency and database cost need a budget. A scan is not a substitute for fixing the publication protocol. Production implication: measure time from admitted state to discoverable eligible work, and alert on tasks stuck in intermediate states. Interview takeaway: durability of intent and repeat-safe reservation are separate from queue delivery semantics.

Dependencies and cancellation

A dependency becomes satisfied only after its accepted output is durably published and accessible to the next task. A container exit event can arrive before its output object is verified. Publish immutable output under an attempt identity, then conditionally promote a pointer for the current attempt. Cancellation records a generation or intent that future transitions must check. It cannot guarantee that an external action already in flight was undone. Expose cancellation requested versus cancellation observed, and retain outcome uncertainty when necessary.

Chapter summary

Persist intent before acknowledgment, make work discovery repeatable, and conditionally advance authoritative task state. Queue duplication is manageable when it cannot bypass reservations or logical identity.

03 · Resource models and placement

Fit the workload that actually runs

Resource arithmetic needs memory, topology, isolation, and enforceable reservations.

Filter before scoring

A resource request is a vector: CPU, host memory, GPU count and type, GPU memory, local storage, network capabilities, and sometimes licenses or locality constraints. Some resources are divisible and compressible; others are exclusive or difficult to share predictably. A GPU count without model compatibility and memory requirements is not enough to determine feasibility. Distinguish physical capacity, allocatable capacity after system reserves, committed reservations, and observed utilization.

Filtering eliminates nodes that violate hard requirements: insufficient resources, wrong accelerator, unsupported network, tenant isolation, unavailable data, or policy restrictions. Scoring ranks feasible candidates using soft preferences such as locality, packing, balanced failure domains, or energy cost. A high score cannot override a hard safety constraint. Kubernetes documents a scheduling framework with distinct extension phases; the lesson's simplified filter-and-reserve protocol is a conceptual design rather than a reimplementation of every current plugin.

Observed low utilization does not make a reserved resource free. A training process can be temporarily idle during input loading while still owning its GPUs. Opportunistic overcommit is possible only with a defined eviction and isolation contract. Likewise, a claimed 20 GB memory request that actually consumes 60 GB can harm colocated jobs unless the platform enforces limits and handles violations. Resource declaration and resource enforcement are different responsibilities.

Worked example 2: aggregate capacity but no feasible node

Initial state: node A has eight free CPUs, 24 GB free host memory, and one suitable GPU. Node B has sixteen free CPUs, 12 GB free memory, and one suitable GPU. A task requires four CPUs, 20 GB memory, and one GPU on a single node. The objective is a feasible placement without splitting an indivisible task. A passes all three resource checks. B fails memory even though the cluster total is 36 GB. The scheduler reserves A, subtracting its request from A's allocatable remainder.

After reservation, A has four CPUs and 4 GB memory remaining with zero free GPUs. A second identical task cannot fit there, and B still fails memory. Aggregate two-GPU availability before the first placement did not imply two feasible tasks. This is fragmentation across resource dimensions. The intermediate reservation must be visible to competing schedulers before either worker launches; otherwise both may consume the same nominal free GPU.

Failure variation: A's health status becomes stale and its GPU is quarantined after scoring. The conditional reservation verifies current resource revision and availability before commitment. If the revision changed, the scheduler refreshes or tries another feasible node. Dispatch may still fail after a valid reservation because health observations cannot be perfectly current. Reconciliation releases or replaces the reservation only under a protocol that prevents the old attempt from continuing to use a resource reassigned to another tenant.

Final outcome is one reserved task on A, not two jobs promised from aggregate arithmetic. Production implication: expose unschedulable reasons by resource and constraint, and distinguish temporarily unavailable capacity from structurally infeasible requests. An autoscaler can add the right node shape for the former, but blindly adding more wrong-shaped nodes does not fix the latter. Interview takeaway: feasibility is per placement unit and per topology, not merely the sum of cluster resources.

Complexity and practical heuristics

General multidimensional packing with constraints is computationally difficult, so production schedulers use heuristics and bounded search. A naive pass evaluates every candidate for each pending task, costing roughly tasks times nodes times constraints. Index candidate pools by hardware and policy, cache immutable attributes, and invalidate dynamic resource state carefully. Sampling can reduce search cost but may miss rare feasible placements. Report the policy and its quality tradeoff rather than promising optimal utilization from a greedy score.

Chapter summary

Model allocatable vectors and hard constraints, then score feasible choices. Reserve against current state before dispatch, and explain fragmentation instead of equating aggregate free capacity with usable placement.

04 · Concurrency and reservations

Two schedulers must not promise the same GPU

A candidate snapshot is a proposal; a committed reservation is authority.

Optimistic scheduling and transactional commitment

Multiple scheduler workers can compute candidates in parallel from snapshots. That improves throughput but permits races. The safe boundary is a transaction or equivalent conditional reservation that checks current capacity, task revision, and ownership before committing. In-memory assumptions can avoid local duplicate work, but they cannot alone protect against another scheduler process or a restart. If reservations span several metadata partitions, identify how all-or-nothing commitment is achieved or what partial states require recovery.

One simple architecture assigns a capacity partition to a single scheduling authority backed by a durable log. Another allows optimistic concurrency with conditional updates and retries. The first simplifies ordering but may bottleneck or complicate failover; the second improves parallelism but can waste work under contention. Choose based on placement rate, resource scope, and the cost of failed proposals. Do not use a global lock for every telemetry update just because allocations require serialization.

Reservations need a lifecycle: tentative, committed, dispatched, running, releasing, and released or expired. A reservation timeout can reclaim a scheduler's tentative hold, but reclaiming a resource from a running worker requires exclusion or reliable termination evidence. Separate tentative hold expiry from execution lease expiry. Otherwise a paused worker can continue consuming a GPU that the metadata now promises to someone else.

Worked example 3: stale snapshots race

Initial state: node N has one free GPU and resource revision 30. Tasks X and Y are both ready. Scheduler S1 proposes X on N from revision 30; scheduler S2 proposes Y on the same snapshot. The objective is at most one committed allocation for that GPU. S1 submits a transaction checking N revision 30, free GPU count at least one, and X still ready. It commits X's attempt A1 and the reservation, sets free count to zero, and advances N to revision 31.

S2's transaction expecting revision 30 fails. It must not dispatch Y from the old proposal. It refreshes N and selects another node or leaves Y pending. Intermediate state includes two proposals but one committed reservation. If S1 crashes after committing and before dispatch, a dispatcher or reconciler discovers the committed reservation and sends the same attempt identity. The transaction must not require the original scheduler process to remain alive for liveness.

Failure variation: the worker starts A1 but its acknowledgment is lost. Repeated dispatch of A1 should return or adopt the same attempt under the worker's durable identity contract, not launch a second process. If the worker cannot make that guarantee, the controller needs an observable runtime ID and a reconciliation procedure before allocating another process. A queue's deduplication setting is insufficient because the uncertainty exists between controller and runtime.

Final outcome: X owns the GPU under A1, and Y remains unallocated until another commitment succeeds. Correctness comes from the transactional resource and task check plus runtime enforcement, not from which scheduler scored the node first. Production implication: monitor reservation conflict rate, committed-but-not-dispatched age, and orphan runtime processes. Interview takeaway: parallel proposal computation and serialized conflict resolution can coexist, but the exact commitment boundary must be named.

Partition and recovery behavior

If the metadata authority is unreachable, existing workers may keep running under their valid execution contract while new allocations stop. That avoids turning a control-plane outage into a full workload outage. A replacement scheduler reconstructs reservations from durable state and reconciles workers before freeing uncertain resources. The recovery inventory must include processes not represented in the latest cache. Reconstructing desired state alone without observing actual resource use can cause unsafe double assignment.

Chapter summary

Proposals can be concurrent and stale. Commitment must atomically establish resource and task ownership, and dispatch must preserve the committed attempt identity through failures.

05 · Leases, attempts, and outputs

A timed-out worker may still be working

Use incarnation-aware authority and conditional publication rather than assuming absence.

Lease ownership and the external boundary

A lease grants authority for a bounded period according to a specified clock and renewal contract. Heartbeats provide observations of liveness; they are not proof that an unreachable worker has stopped. A process can pause, lose network access, or continue computing behind a partition. If the scheduler creates a successor, the system may temporarily have two physical attempts even while it intends one logical result.

Assign a unique attempt ID and increasing task generation. The output store accepts publication only if the attempt matches the task's current authorized generation, and the task has not been canceled or completed incompatibly. A worker writes immutable output under its attempt identity, then requests conditional promotion of the logical output pointer. A stale worker can create an orphan object, but it cannot replace the accepted result. Garbage collection later removes unreferenced objects under a retention policy.

This technique establishes at most one accepted result under the atomic publication contract. It does not establish that computation occurred once, that no money was spent twice, or that external tools were executed once. Effectful tasks need stable business action keys, provider lookup, or supported deduplication. If an old worker has unrestricted access to an external API that ignores the scheduler generation, the scheduler cannot fence that effect merely by rejecting its final output.

Worked example 4: old attempt returns after replacement

Initial state: task T runs as attempt A7 with generation 7. Its execution lease is valid until the authority's time 10:00:30. The worker last renewed at 10:00:10, then becomes partitioned. The objective is safe completion publication if a replacement is needed. At 10:00:31, the controller records the old attempt as uncertain, applies the defined exclusion policy, and authorizes A8 at generation 8. The exact timing is illustrative; production must account for the lease protocol's clock and drift assumptions.

A8 computes the result and writes immutable object O8. It conditionally publishes O8 while the authoritative task generation is 8 and state is running. The transaction changes the task to succeeded and records the accepted output. A7 resumes and writes O7. Its publication request includes generation 7 and is rejected as stale. Intermediate state has two physical output objects, but the logical pointer names only O8. Final outcome is one accepted logical result, with O7 eligible for later cleanup.

Failure variation: A7 called an external billing API before the partition, and its response was lost. Rejecting O7 does not undo that charge. A8 must use the same logical billing action identity and query or replay under the provider's actual contract before creating a new charge. If no safe reconciliation exists, pause the effectful branch. This is a safety-versus-liveness choice at an external boundary.

Another variation is the controller freeing A7's GPU while A7 can still execute. Output fencing protects the logical result but not resource isolation. Reassigning the same physical device requires verified termination, runtime-enforced exclusion, or another supported isolation mechanism. Production implication: separate result fencing, effect fencing, and resource reclamation. Interview takeaway: “at most one accepted result” is a precise achievable claim; “exactly one execution” is usually a much stronger and different claim.

Retry budgets and backoff

Classify failures before retrying. A transient worker loss may justify another attempt; an invalid model format or permission denial often will not improve by retrying unchanged. Bound attempts, total elapsed time, and resource spend, not just retries per process. Use jittered backoff to avoid synchronized replacement storms, but preserve urgent recovery priorities through a separate policy. Publish a terminal explanation with failure evidence so operators can distinguish exhausted retries from a permanent specification error.

Chapter summary

Leases guide ownership, generations fence accepted results, and external contracts protect business effects. Safe resource reclamation requires its own evidence that a stale process cannot keep using the resource.

06 · Gang scheduling and topology

Reserve the useful group, not a collection of stranded tasks

Cooperating ranks need resource-shape readiness and coordinated startup.

All-or-nothing reservation versus simultaneous execution

A distributed training attempt may require eight workers with eight GPUs each on a compatible fabric. Launching half the workers can consume scarce capacity while producing no useful progress. Gang scheduling treats the required group as one reservation decision. Atomic reservation does not mean every process starts at precisely the same instant. A startup barrier verifies membership, connectivity, identity, and readiness before the job begins meaningful collective work.

Ray's placement-group documentation describes atomic resource-group reservation and placement strategies. That gives a concrete public mechanism to compare with the underlying requirement. Its versioned details and supported policies should be checked before operational use. A scheduler still needs to decide which gangs enter the queue, how long reservations may wait for startup, what happens when one member fails, and how topology affects feasibility.

Topology constraints can include same-node tensor-parallel groups, same-fabric training ranks, anti-affinity across failure domains, and data-local preprocessing. Packing improves communication locality and may reduce the number of jobs affected by a host loss. Spreading can reduce correlated failures for independent replicas. Neither is universally best. The useful placement unit is determined by the application's communication and recovery semantics, not by a generic spread preference.

Worked example 5: partial gangs deadlock the fleet

Initial state: the cluster has eight suitable GPUs. Job J1 and job J2 each require a gang of six. A naive scheduler gives four GPUs to J1 and four to J2, then each waits for two more. The objective is useful progress rather than nominal allocated utilization. Every GPU is reserved, yet neither job can start. This is a hold-and-wait pattern created by partial reservations with no completion plan.

Under an all-or-nothing group reservation, the scheduler chooses one eligible job according to policy, say J1, reserves six, and leaves two free for compatible independent work. J2 remains pending without holding four stranded GPUs. The startup coordinator launches J1's six ranks with one attempt generation, checks all members, and opens the barrier only when the group is ready. Final outcome is one useful six-GPU job rather than two blocked fragments.

Failure variation: one rank fails during startup after five have loaded large state. The policy can retry the missing rank within a bounded startup window if the framework supports that membership model, or terminate the whole attempt and release its group safely. It must not leave the reservation permanently occupying capacity. Record which resources are still in use while cleanup is incomplete. A cancellation acknowledgment from the controller is not proof that every runtime process has exited.

Another variation is enough aggregate GPUs in two incompatible fabrics. A gang requiring one low-latency domain remains infeasible even if the count is sufficient. The autoscaler needs a shape-aware request, not just six arbitrary GPUs. Production implication: measure group queue time, startup barrier time, partial-start cleanup, and topology fragmentation separately. Interview takeaway: gang scheduling protects useful progress, but it can increase queueing and head-of-line blocking unless paired with careful backfill and admission.

Backfill without starving large work

A large queued gang may wait for resources while small jobs fit in gaps. Backfill can improve utilization if those small jobs finish before a reserved start time or can be safely preempted. If duration estimates are inaccurate, backfill can delay the large job indefinitely. Use explicit reservations, aging, or priority policy and report its limitations. A scheduler should distinguish “cannot ever fit this pool” from “can fit after current allocations finish,” because these require different user guidance and capacity actions.

Chapter summary

Atomic group reservation avoids stranded partial allocations. Startup, topology, membership changes, cleanup, and fair backfill remain separate mechanisms needed for useful gang execution.

07 · Fairness and preemption

Optimize useful utilization under a declared sharing policy

Priority, quota, and fairness answer different questions.

Fairness over multiple resources

A tenant quota sets an entitlement or upper bound. Priority chooses relative urgency. Fairness determines how contested resources are shared among eligible demand. A high-priority label should not automatically bypass quota, tenant isolation, or safety constraints. If customers can freely mark every job urgent, the priority mechanism becomes meaningless. Define who may assign priority and how its cost is attributed.

Dominant resource share is the largest fraction of any resource consumed by a tenant. The DRF research provides a principled multi-resource fairness model under its assumptions. In practice, GPU types are not necessarily interchangeable, placement constraints make feasible allocations discrete, and historical usage can matter. A simple dominant-share calculation is useful for explaining policy, but it is not a complete production allocator with every formal DRF property.

Suppose the allocatable pool has 100 CPUs and ten GPUs. Tenant A currently uses twenty CPUs and four GPUs, so its shares are 0.2 and 0.4 and dominant share is 0.4. Tenant B uses thirty CPUs and one GPU, so its dominant share is max(0.3,0.1), or 0.3. A simplified equal-weight policy may prefer feasible additional demand from B before A. That preference still depends on the next request's shape and any entitlement or priority override. Comparing current shares alone cannot guarantee that a proposed placement is feasible.

Preemption has a recovery cost

Preemption means taking resources from running work for a higher-priority purpose under an explicit policy. For a checkpointable training job, the cost includes time since the last valid checkpoint, checkpoint overhead if one is requested, termination and cleanup, replacement startup, and the victim's later queue delay. For an effectful agent task, stopping midway may leave an unknown business action that requires reconciliation. Treat these as costs and safety constraints rather than assuming every container can be killed interchangeably.

Consider a 64-GPU job that has run ten minutes since its last complete checkpoint. Killing it may discard up to 640 GPU-minutes of computation, before restart costs. If a new checkpoint requires two blocking minutes, that costs 128 GPU-minutes of occupied time but may preserve the ten-minute interval. Whether to wait depends on emergency deadlines, checkpoint success likelihood, storage contention, and whether the urgent job truly needs all 64 GPUs. The arithmetic is a scenario comparison, not a universal rule to checkpoint before every eviction.

Preemption can also create cascading load: many victims checkpoint simultaneously, saturate storage, then all restart and reload data later. Bound concurrency and separate emergency capacity from opportunistic reclaimable work when the contract requires predictable start times. A reservation policy may cost steady-state utilization but avoid expensive surprise eviction. Show the business objective that justifies the reserve.

Starvation, aging, and honest accounting

Strict priority can starve lower classes indefinitely under sustained high-priority load. Aging or weighted service can improve progress guarantees, but nonpreemptible long jobs still complicate latency bounds. Define whether fairness applies to running allocation, cumulative resource-time, or completed useful work. A tenant with repeatedly failing jobs can consume a large share without producing results; charge attempts and waste explicitly so failure does not become a way to evade cost attribution.

How I would explain this in an interview: “I distinguish what a tenant is entitled to, how urgent this job is, and how shared contention is resolved. For AI workloads, the cost of preemption is often measured in lost accelerator-time and restart delay. I choose a fairness and reserve policy with those costs visible.”

Chapter summary

Fairness is a policy over declared resources and time, not a synonym for FIFO. Preemption must account for wasted work, restart cost, effect uncertainty, and the risk of synchronized recovery storms.

08 · Queue stability and scheduler scale

Keep recovery and admission from overwhelming the control plane

A queue can absorb bursts, but it cannot make an unstable workload disappear.

Arrival rate, service demand, and queueing

For a stable system observed over a suitable interval, Little's Law relates average number of jobs in the system L, arrival rate λ, and average time in the system W: L = λW. Define the boundary consistently. If W includes waiting and execution, L includes waiting and running jobs. It is not a formula for the p99, and it does not create stability when offered load exceeds sustainable service. Use it as a measurement consistency check and rough capacity guide.

Worked example 6 begins with ten jobs per second and an average end-to-end duration of thirty seconds. Under stable conditions, the average in-system population is 300 jobs. If the average execution time is twenty seconds and each job occupies one worker for its whole execution, the average running population is 200 and the average waiting population is 100. Those averages do not tell you how many workers are needed for a tail-latency target, because duration variance, bursts, retries, and scheduling constraints still matter.

Now suppose only 150 equivalent workers are available and jobs still require twenty seconds each on average. The rough maximum completion rate is 7.5 jobs per second under the simplified one-job-per-worker model. With ten arriving, backlog grows by about 2.5 jobs per second, or 9,000 per hour, before other effects. The objective becomes restoring stability through more usable capacity, reduced admitted demand, or less work per job. A longer queue merely delays failure and increases latency.

Failure variation: a worker outage causes mass retries, increasing effective arrivals. If every failed job immediately requeues, retry traffic can dominate new work and block recovery. Use retry budgets, jitter, and admission by workload class. Reserve some control-plane and execution capacity for reconciliation so the system can discover completed work and free resources. Final outcome is a bounded and observable queue under a sustainable admitted load. Interview takeaway: separate offered demand, admitted demand, attempts, and useful completions.

Partition the scheduler carefully

Partition by tenant or resource pool to scale decisions, but consider cross-partition placements. A large gang spanning pools can require coordinated reservations and can become stuck if each authority independently holds partial resources. Hierarchical scheduling can allocate coarse capacity shares to tenant schedulers while a lower layer enforces physical resources. The hierarchy needs reclaim, expiry, and reconciliation semantics. It is not enough to divide a global count into spreadsheets.

Control-plane state also has different value densities. Allocation and accepted completion are authoritative; high-frequency telemetry can often be sampled, coalesced, or transported asynchronously. Do not put every GPU utilization sample through the same transaction path as ownership changes. OpenAI's historical Kubernetes account describes bursty large-job creation, and Google Borg and Meta Twine publications establish real large-scale orchestration contexts. Our scale assumptions and queue design remain hypothetical rather than inferred company internals.

Observe scheduling cycle latency, candidate count, reservation conflicts, unschedulable reason, queue age by class, startup readiness, lease uncertainty, orphan processes, and useful resource-time. A single cluster utilization percentage hides fragmentation, unfairness, and expensive failed attempts. Choose metrics that explain why admitted work is not progressing and which invariant prevents it from starting.

Chapter summary

Queues buffer finite bursts; stable service requires sufficient usable capacity and controlled attempts. Scale authoritative decisions separately from telemetry, and retain a recovery path for resource and task reconciliation.

09 · Production scheduler architecture

Build a recoverable multi-tenant execution service

A complete hypothetical design for batch AI tasks and urgent recovery workflows.

Requirements, APIs, and data ownership

Design a platform accepting 2,000 job submissions per second at peak, with up to one million pending tasks, ten thousand workers, and several accelerator pools. Assume most embedding tasks are independent, some training jobs require gangs, and a small class of authorized disaster-recovery jobs needs protected admission. Tenant isolation, durable acceptance, bounded retries, and at most one accepted task result are mandatory. External effect safety is negotiated per tool provider. The system should keep running safe existing work during a temporary scheduler outage.

The submission API accepts tenant identity, request key, immutable job specification, resource vectors, dependencies, permitted pools, execution policy, and optional deadline. Return a durable job ID after persistence and discovery intent commit. A status API separates accepted, pending eligibility, unschedulable, reserved, running, retrying, and terminal outcomes. Cancellation creates a durable cancellation request with a generation, then reports which attempts have actually stopped or remain uncertain. Avoid treating an asynchronous acknowledgment as immediate physical termination.

The architecture has an admission service, authoritative metadata store, durable discovery/outbox path, eligibility controller, scheduling workers, reservation authority, dispatcher, worker agents, output publication service, and reconciliation controllers. These can share infrastructure when the chosen transaction boundary permits it. Keep allocation decisions distinct from observation streams. A capacity inventory records allocatable vectors, health revision, topology, and committed reservations; a telemetry pipeline records observed utilization without rewriting every allocation row.

Persist job and task identities, specification revision, dependency counters or verified edges, active attempt generation, reservation, worker incarnation, lease, retry budget, output reference, and cancellation state. Partition by resource authority or task ownership with explicit cross-partition protocols. A transaction accepting a reservation should check task readiness and resource availability together within its supported scope. If that scope is too large, use staged reservations with a recovery protocol rather than hand-waving atomicity across unrelated stores.

Normal execution and crash recovery

Admission validates policy and feasibility, persists intent, and publishes discovery repeatably. Eligibility waits for accepted dependency outputs and retry time. A scheduler filters candidates, scores feasible placements, and conditionally commits a reservation and attempt. The dispatcher sends the same attempt identity until the worker's outcome is known. Worker agents enforce local resource and sandbox limits, report runtime identity, and renew their execution contract. A completed attempt writes immutable output and asks the publication service to atomically validate generation, cancellation, and current task state before accepting the result.

If the scheduler crashes before reservation, its proposal is disposable. After reservation but before dispatch, durable intent drives successor dispatch. After dispatch but before acknowledgment, the successor queries the worker or runtime by attempt identity. After output write but before publication, retrying publication is conditional and repeat-safe. After publication but before the client sees success, the status API returns the accepted result. Trace these boundaries because every one creates a different recovery action.

For a failed worker, the reconciler distinguishes unreachable from proven stopped. It can mark the task uncertain, fence result publication, and authorize a new attempt under policy, but physical resource reuse still needs safe exclusion. A worker returning with an old incarnation cannot renew another worker's lease or publish for a newer attempt. Reconcile orphan runtimes and abandoned reservations after leader changes. Maintenance drains should stop new assignments first, then checkpoint or terminate work according to its contract.

Scale, isolation, operations, and alternatives

At 2,000 submissions per second, one database transaction per submission may be reasonable or not depending on task fan-out and the actual store. If the average submission creates 100 tasks, metadata creation is 200,000 task records per second before state transitions. Bound fan-out, batch where supported, and avoid synchronously creating an unbounded graph in one request. If ten thousand workers heartbeat every ten seconds, the baseline is 1,000 heartbeats per second, with recovery bursts above that. These are hypothetical estimates to drive load testing, not datastore performance claims.

Workers run tenant code in an isolation boundary appropriate to trust: containers alone may be insufficient for hostile workloads, and GPU sharing needs hardware and runtime support. Scope credentials to job, attempt, and permitted data; do not place a fleet-wide administrator credential in every worker. Validate object references and dependency edges to prevent cross-tenant data access. The disaster-recovery class needs authorized priority, protected provider budgets, and audit, not a general customer-controlled priority escalation.

Observe accepted-to-start latency by class, useful completion throughput, deadline misses, reservation conflicts, retry spend, orphan count, and isolation violations. SLOs should distinguish control-plane acceptance from execution start and completion. Runbooks include freezing admission, draining a pool, pausing retries, recovering metadata authority, reconciling unknown effects, and cleaning abandoned attempts safely. A replayable simulation can compare scheduling policy changes before rollout, but production canaries still need real workload evidence.

Compare a single scheduling authority, optimistic parallel schedulers, and a hierarchy of pool and tenant schedulers. Start with the simplest model that meets measured decision throughput and failure isolation. Evolve when reservation contention, candidate search, or authority recovery becomes limiting. Preserve API and durable identities during migration so a new scheduler can adopt existing jobs instead of duplicating them. The architectural defense is a chain of invariants and recovery traces, not the largest component diagram.

Chapter summary

The production service must recover intent, reservations, runtime identity, and publication consistently. Scaling decisions must preserve those boundaries rather than moving correctness into unverifiable caches.

10 · Implementation and verification

Model the reservation race and stale completion

An executable small model makes authority checks concrete without pretending to be a distributed datastore.

Conditional operations in a teaching model

The Python example implements a serial in-memory resource reservation with an expected revision and a generation-checked output publication. Two callers read revision zero and both want the only GPU. The first reservation succeeds and advances the revision. The second fails because it uses a stale revision. A publication from attempt generation 7 is rejected when the current generation is 8. These checks demonstrate the predicates a real transactional implementation must enforce.

The model is executable with the standard library. It is not thread-safe and does not implement persistence, clocks, leases, worker termination, or network faults. A production equivalent must place the checks and updates inside one supported atomic boundary. Translating the example into separate read and write calls would reintroduce the race. An assertion passing in a serial process does not prove a database implementation is linearizable; it makes the intended state transition precise enough to test.

PYTHON · executable standard-library model
class Resource:
    def __init__(self, free):
        if free < 0:
            raise ValueError('negative capacity')
        self.free = free
        self.revision = 0

    def reserve(self, expected_revision, amount):
        if amount <= 0:
            raise ValueError('request must be positive')
        if self.revision != expected_revision or self.free < amount:
            return False
        self.free -= amount
        self.revision += 1
        return True

class Task:
    def __init__(self, generation):
        self.generation = generation
        self.output = None

    def publish(self, generation, output):
        if generation != self.generation or self.output is not None:
            return False
        self.output = output
        return True

# Serial model: production checks/updates require an atomic datastore operation.
r = Resource(1)
assert r.reserve(0, 1) is True
assert r.reserve(0, 1) is False
assert r.free == 0
t = Task(8)
assert t.publish(7, 'old-output') is False
assert t.publish(8, 'new-output') is True
assert t.publish(8, 'another-output') is False
assert t.output == 'new-output'
assert 10 * 30 == 300
assert 150 / 20 == 7.5
assert (10 - 7.5) * 3600 == 9000
assert 64 * 10 == 640
print('one reservation; stale proposal rejected; generation-8 result accepted')

The resource check uses both expected revision and available capacity. The revision catches stale proposals, while the capacity predicate expresses the invariant directly. The publication check verifies the current attempt and that no result was already accepted. A real service may return the existing result for a same-identity repeat rather than simply return false; define that API behavior explicitly. Both toy operations take constant time because they access one fixed-size state record. Candidate filtering and cross-resource reservations have different complexity and are not hidden inside this claim.

Expected output is one successful reservation, one rejected stale reservation, and one accepted generation-8 output. Extend the tests by trying a negative request, canceling the task before publication, or simulating two different output identities for the same attempt. Then write the corresponding transaction preconditions. This exercise tests whether you can turn an architectural promise into enforceable state predicates.

Failure injection and validation evidence

Inject a crash at each persistence and effect boundary. Duplicate queue messages, delay worker acknowledgments, pause a worker beyond lease expiry, and resume it after replacement. Verify the accepted output and actual resource inventory, not just task status. Kill one gang member during startup and prove the others either remain within a supported recovery protocol or are cleaned up. Test cancellation while an external provider is returning an uncertain outcome.

Load tests should include burst fan-out, heterogeneous resource shapes, hot tenants, and a region or pool returning with thousands of workers at once. Compare offered submissions, admitted jobs, attempts, and useful completions. A scheduling throughput benchmark that ignores persistence and reconciliation measures only proposal speed. A utilization benchmark that counts blocked partial gangs can reward the wrong behavior. Keep test definitions aligned with the business objective.

For rollout, canary policy changes on a bounded pool or tenant class. Preserve the previous policy and a way to stop new allocations while allowing current work to finish. Do not “roll back” by forgetting committed reservations. If the metadata schema evolves, support old and new controller versions during the transition or drain ownership explicitly. Record which assumptions were tested and which remain hypothetical, including actual GPU isolation and provider effect contracts.

Chapter summary

Executable predicates clarify the intended invariant. Distributed correctness still requires an atomic implementation, runtime enforcement, crash testing, and evidence that resource state matches the controller's records.

11 · Original Senior Staff questions

Defend useful progress under stale observations

Original interview practice; no question is presented as a confirmed company interview.

Twelve scheduling architecture challenges

1. Why is a queue plus workers not a complete scheduler?

Evaluation: scope and invariants. A queue discovers work; admission, resource feasibility, reservations, dependency readiness, fairness, attempts, and completion require additional contracts. Trace an accepted task through durable state and runtime execution. Follow-up: what happens when a message is duplicated? Only an authoritative conditional reservation may authorize an attempt. Common mistake: treating delivery as allocation. Tradeoff: simple worker pools are appropriate for homogeneous independent work, but should not promise unsupported GPU or effect semantics.

2. Two schedulers pick the last GPU. How do you prevent double allocation?

Evaluation: concurrency boundary. Compute proposals concurrently, then atomically check current resource revision, available vector, and task readiness while committing the reservation. A losing proposal refreshes. Runtime enforcement must match the reservation. Follow-up: one scheduler dies after commit. A successor dispatches the durable attempt identity. Common mistake: relying on each process's local lock. Tradeoff: optimistic concurrency scales proposals but can waste search work under heavy contention.

3. Can you guarantee a task executes exactly once?

Evaluation: precise guarantees. Physical computation may repeat after uncertain failure. You can enforce one accepted logical result through generation-checked publication, while external effects require their own provider contract. State which guarantee the product actually needs. Follow-up: the old worker charged a customer before timeout. Reconcile by stable business identity. Common mistake: claiming exactly-once execution from queue settings. Tradeoff: pausing ambiguous effectful work protects safety but can delay completion.

4. Why not free resources immediately when a lease expires?

Evaluation: resource-side safety. The old process may still run. Distinguish metadata authority expiry from verified termination or enforced exclusion. Result fencing does not stop GPU consumption. Quarantine or reclaim through a supported runtime boundary before promising the device again. Follow-up: the worker resumes after a long pause. Demonstrate stale renewal and publication rejection. Common mistake: treating unreachable as stopped. Tradeoff: conservative reclamation can reduce availability while preserving tenant isolation.

5. The fleet has enough free GPUs, but the training job cannot start. Why?

Evaluation: placement shape. Check per-node memory, accelerator compatibility, topology, isolation, and gang requirements. Aggregate count can be fragmented across incompatible nodes or fabrics. Distinguish structurally infeasible from temporarily blocked. Follow-up: what should autoscaling add? Request the required shape, not arbitrary GPUs. Common mistake: equating count with a usable gang. Tradeoff: topology-aware packing improves communication but can constrain sharing and increase queue time.

6. How does gang scheduling avoid two blocked half-jobs?

Evaluation: group reservation and liveness. Reserve the complete useful group or hold none under the selected policy. Then use a startup barrier and bounded cleanup. Two six-GPU jobs should not each hold four of eight while waiting forever. Follow-up: one member fails at startup. Explain supported retry or whole-group termination. Common mistake: calling atomic reservation simultaneous execution. Tradeoff: all-or-nothing groups avoid stranded capacity but can create head-of-line blocking.

7. Should the least-utilized tenant always get the next job?

Evaluation: policy semantics. Define fairness over resources and time, entitlements, priority, and feasible demand. CPU utilization alone ignores GPU share and reservations. A dominant-share model helps compare multiple dimensions but is not a complete allocator with arbitrary constraints. Follow-up: the next job cannot fit. Use another eligible request without abandoning starvation protection. Common mistake: confusing quota with priority. Tradeoff: packing, fairness, and latency may conflict and need a declared hierarchy.

8. Is killing a low-priority training job a cheap way to admit urgent work?

Evaluation: preemption economics. Count lost work since checkpoint, checkpoint cost, cleanup, restart, and later queue delay. A 64-GPU ten-minute interval exposes 640 GPU-minutes before other costs. Respect effectful-task safety. Follow-up: all victims checkpoint together. Bound storage contention. Common mistake: optimizing urgent start time while hiding total waste. Tradeoff: protected reserve costs idle capacity but can provide more predictable emergency service.

9. What does Little's Law tell you, and what does it not?

Evaluation: quantitative judgment. With stable boundaries, L = λW relates averages. Ten jobs per second and thirty seconds in system implies 300 jobs present on average. It does not predict p99 or establish sufficient workers under arbitrary variance. Follow-up: service capacity is 7.5 jobs per second. Backlog grows under ten admitted arrivals. Common mistake: applying steady-state arithmetic to an unstable queue. Tradeoff: admission protects latency but rejects or defers demand.

10. How do you recover a scheduler without killing running jobs?

Evaluation: control/data-plane separation. Reconstruct durable reservations and task attempts, reconcile worker incarnations and runtime IDs, and preserve valid existing execution. Stop new unsafe allocations if authority is unavailable. Do not free resources solely because a cache is empty. Follow-up: a worker reports a process absent from desired state. Quarantine and reconcile the orphan. Common mistake: restarting everything to simplify metadata. Tradeoff: reconciliation complexity preserves expensive useful work.

11. Can cancellation guarantee that a cloud action never happened?

Evaluation: effect uncertainty. Cancellation prevents future permitted transitions and requests runtime stop; it cannot erase an already-accepted external operation. Record requested versus observed cancellation and reconcile unknown effects with stable IDs. Follow-up: a provider response arrives after cancellation. Preserve and classify the outcome rather than discard evidence. Common mistake: equating canceled status with rollback. Tradeoff: user simplicity must not hide irreversible partial progress.

12. How do you scale decisions without a global lock?

Evaluation: partition and coordination scope. Partition resource authority, use bounded parallel proposal search, and commit conflicts within explicit transactional scopes. Keep telemetry off the allocation critical path. Cross-pool gangs need a real reservation protocol. Follow-up: one hot tenant spans pools. Explain fairness and movement. Common mistake: claiming independent shards make every placement atomic. Tradeoff: partitioning improves throughput and isolation while complicating global optimization and large groups.

Architecture defense: emergency work during a retry storm

The interviewer removes twenty percent of workers while urgent DR jobs arrive and a large tenant floods retries. Clarify emergency authorization, effect safety, protected capacity, and which jobs can be preempted. A strong design bounds retry budgets, reserves reconciliation throughput, admits emergency work within explicit limits, and checkpoints or terminates eligible victims without forgetting live reservations. It reports partial capacity honestly and avoids launching duplicate cloud actions. The model solution separates policy from invariants: priority changes which eligible work wins; it does not waive tenant isolation, resource exclusion, or uncertain-effect reconciliation.

Chapter summary

A strong defense traces state transitions under failure, quantifies useful progress, and names the enforcement boundary. A component list without recovery semantics is not a scheduling architecture.

12 · Speaking and recall

Explain the scheduler as a correctness protocol

Connect resource efficiency to durable authority and useful business completion.

Thirty-second explanation

A distributed scheduler turns durable job intent into safe resource reservations and recoverable execution. I separate admission, eligibility, placement, reservation, dispatch, and accepted completion. Proposals may be stale and workers may return after timeout. Conditional reservations prevent double allocation; attempt generations protect publication; external effects need their own identity contract. Fairness and utilization operate inside those safety boundaries.

Two-minute explanation

I would start with workload classes and invariants. Independent embedding shards, interactive inference, training gangs, and effectful recovery jobs need different latency, topology, retry, and safety contracts. A queue discovers eligible work, but the authoritative store preserves jobs, attempts, reservations, and outcomes.

Schedulers filter hard constraints and score feasible placements from snapshots. Before dispatch, a transaction checks current resource state and task revision, then commits a reservation and attempt identity. A crash after that point is recovered from durable intent. Duplicate notifications and dispatches reuse the same identity rather than creating unrelated work.

Workers enforce resource and sandbox limits. Heartbeats and leases provide liveness evidence, but an unreachable worker may still run. A new attempt generation can reject stale output publication, while physical resource reuse needs verified exclusion. External tool actions require provider-side deduplication or reconciliation; one accepted result does not mean one physical execution.

For scale, I partition authority, keep telemetry off the allocation critical path, and bound retries and admission. Gang reservations avoid blocked half-jobs. Fairness and preemption account for accelerator-time waste and restart cost. I would validate the design by crashing every transition boundary and measuring useful completion, not just queue delivery or allocated utilization.

Five-minute explanation

Suppose we are building a multi-tenant AI execution service. I would first separate logical jobs, schedulable tasks, and execution attempts. The user's request should have a durable stable identity even when messages repeat and workers restart. I would define latency classes, resource shapes, isolation, retry budgets, and external-effect contracts before selecting a scheduling policy.

Submission would atomically persist the immutable specification and a discovery intent. An outbox or equivalent mechanism would make work discoverable despite publication crashes. The authoritative task state would retain dependencies, eligibility time, active generation, reservation, and cancellation. A periodic reconciler would detect stuck states and missing observations without treating a cache as the source of truth.

Placement would begin by filtering hard constraints: allocatable CPU and memory, accelerator type and memory, topology, tenant policy, and data access. Scoring would rank feasible nodes for locality or packing. Multiple scheduling workers could compute proposals concurrently, but a supported atomic reservation would check current resource and task revisions before committing. Two stale proposals for the same GPU must not both become allocations.

The dispatcher would send the committed attempt identity. If its acknowledgment is lost, recovery would query or repeat the same identity under the worker contract. A process exit alone would not publish a result. The worker would write immutable output and conditionally promote it only if its generation is still current and the task permits completion. A resumed stale worker could leave an orphan object but could not replace the accepted result.

I would distinguish that result guarantee from resource and effect safety. A lease timeout does not prove the process stopped using a GPU. Reassignment needs runtime exclusion or verified termination. A cloud API call may have succeeded before the worker disappeared, so the next attempt must reconcile the same logical action. If the provider cannot support a safe retry, the system should pause instead of claiming exactly-once behavior.

Training gangs add a group reservation and startup contract. Reserving four GPUs each for two six-GPU jobs on an eight-GPU pool produces no useful work. I would reserve a complete group or leave it pending, then validate its members and topology before opening the barrier. Backfill can improve utilization, but needs starvation protection and safe preemption. Preemption cost includes checkpoint age, lost accelerator-time, cleanup, and restart.

For scale, I would partition resource authority and separate allocation state from high-volume telemetry. I would measure queue age, accepted-to-start latency, reservation conflict, startup time, retry spend, orphan processes, and useful completions. Little's Law can check average populations under stable conditions, but it does not predict p99 or make an overloaded queue stable. Rollouts would canary policy changes while preserving existing reservations and durable identities. The architecture is credible when every crash boundary has a recovery action and every efficiency claim respects the safety contract.

Important concepts and final cheat sheet

  • Job: durable logical request.
  • Task: schedulable unit with dependencies.
  • Attempt: one execution incarnation.
  • Admission: policy and feasibility acceptance.
  • Eligibility: prerequisites and retry time satisfied.
  • Placement: proposed resource assignment.
  • Reservation: committed resource ownership.
  • Generation: fence against stale completion.
  • Reconciliation: resolve desired versus observed state.
  • Gang: cooperating group requiring useful joint placement.
  • Dominant share: largest normalized resource consumption.
  • Preemption: policy-governed reclaim with recovery cost.
  • Queue stability: sustainable admitted work under capacity.
  • Useful progress: accepted business work rather than raw allocation.

Common mistakes are assuming delivery equals allocation, lease expiry equals termination, a result fence protects arbitrary tools, or a full GPU fleet means every gang fits. Replace these statements with the exact state predicate, resource shape, and external contract. Natural interview phrases include “the commitment boundary is,” “this observation is stale but not authoritative,” and “the safety invariant prevents that transition.”

Whiteboard and speaking practice

In thirty seconds, distinguish a task from an attempt. In two minutes, trace two schedulers competing for one GPU and identify the atomic check. In five minutes, recover the platform after a scheduler crash and a worker partition without killing every healthy job. On a whiteboard, draw intent persistence, reservation, dispatch, runtime execution, and publication. Inject a crash after every arrow. State what the successor knows, what remains uncertain, and which action is safe to retry.

sd-011 · Reliability foundations

Recover the service, not just the process

Define the business outcome before choosing replication or failover machinery.

Start at the customer boundary

A regional outage interrupts an enterprise assistant. Its HTTP endpoint returns again after four minutes, yet the assistant cannot read its retrieval index, decrypt stored conversations, or look up whether a payment tool already ran. Calling this a four-minute recovery hides the customer's actual experience. The engineering problem is to restore a specified business capability with an acceptable amount of lost state, under an explicitly named failure. That capability might be reading existing conversations, accepting new durable requests, or completing financial actions. Each has a different recovery contract.

High availability concerns continued useful service through expected faults. Disaster recovery concerns restoring service and state after a disruptive failure that exceeds the normal operating envelope. These are engineering scopes, not mutually exclusive product categories. A regional deployment can have excellent node-level availability and no credible regional recovery. Conversely, a well-tested nightly restore may protect data while still permitting an unacceptable day-long interruption.

Start by defining the unit of success. For an inference request, receiving headers is insufficient if the stream stops before meaningful output. For a recovery workflow, accepting a plan is insufficient if no resources transition. A valid measurement includes response correctness, latency, authorization, and the durable effects promised by the API. Separate administrative availability from workload availability: an unavailable deployment console should not necessarily stop already-running models.

The user’s OCI Full Stack Disaster Recovery background is a useful bridge. Think in terms of a business stack and its prerequisites, then extend that reasoning to model artifacts, GPU capacity, retrieval snapshots, policy versions, and tool-effect records. The examples in this lesson are hypothetical designs, not claims about any employer's implementation or your personal accomplishments.

Choose the failure model and the success predicate

List failures independently: a process crashes, a rack loses power, a zone becomes unreachable, a region loses its control plane, an operator deletes a database, or credentials are compromised. A copy across two zones may survive one zone loss but reproduce a malicious delete perfectly. Independence must be argued from actual dependencies. Two regions can share an identity system, deployment pipeline, DNS authority, encryption root, or logical bug. Geographic distance alone does not break those correlations.

For each failure, write a success predicate that can be evaluated by a test. One example is: within fifteen minutes of a region becoming unusable, an authorized customer can retrieve the last acknowledged conversation event and start an approved model request in the surviving region, at the contracted degraded throughput. This statement names an observer, a deadline, a state guarantee, and capacity. It also forces an answer about which acknowledged events survive.

Define the invariant separately from the objective. An invariant could say that two regions must never concurrently authorize conflicting destructive recovery steps for the same protection group. The recovery deadline is an objective; it does not justify violating that invariant. When failure evidence is incomplete, the system may need to wait for fencing rather than become available unsafely. Make that tradeoff explicit before an incident forces it.

How I would explain this in an interview: “I first define what customers must be able to do after a specific failure. Then I identify the authoritative state and the safety boundary. Only after that do I choose redundancy, replication, and orchestration. A green process is evidence about one component; recovery is evidence about the whole business path.” Pause and restate this using a hypothetical DR plan from your own domain.

Chapter summary

Availability is useful service under a defined workload. Recovery is a tested transition to that service after a defined failure. Protect both the business capability and the invariants that make the capability safe.

02 · Quantitative objectives

Make RPO and RTO measurable

Separate acceptable loss, observed loss, and the time needed to restore useful service.

Recovery point is a state boundary

Recovery point objective, or RPO, expresses the acceptable loss of previously committed business state, often as a time interval. Define which commits and which clock it refers to. “Five-minute RPO” is ambiguous if an API acknowledges a request before its database transaction commits, or if the recovered database contains records whose referenced objects never reached the recovery region. A durable checkpoint or replication watermark should identify the complete, usable state boundary rather than merely the latest byte received.

Distinguish received, persisted, applied, and externally usable replication positions. A remote receiver may have durable log segments but still need replay before reads work. If the region fails at 12:10:00 and the latest recoverable committed frontier corresponds to 12:08:40, the observed temporal gap is eighty seconds. It is not automatically the number of lost transactions. Traffic may be bursty, and some later writes may have reached remote storage. When clocks are uncertain, use sequence numbers for correctness and report a conservative time bound with clock uncertainty.

Zero RPO is always relative to a fault model and an acknowledgment rule. A write acknowledged only after durable copies in two independent regions can survive loss of either region under those assumptions. It does not survive arbitrary simultaneous destruction, replicated corruption, or a lost decryption key. If the system silently falls back to local-only acknowledgments during a partition, it has changed the guarantee. Expose that mode transition instead of continuing to advertise zero loss.

Worked example 1: a recovery budget

Initial state: region A accepts 400 committed workflow events per second, and region B has a durable complete frontier eighty seconds behind. The objective is RPO at most sixty seconds and RTO at most fifteen minutes for starting safe recovery plans. At the regional failure, A stops responding. The controller records the last trusted B frontier and prevents further writes under A's ownership epoch. B replays its complete log, checks dependencies, and admits a canary plan before reopening ordinary traffic.

The potential event exposure under a constant-rate approximation is 400 multiplied by 80, or 32,000 events. That estimate is not a proof of which records are missing. Reconciliation compares the acknowledgment ledger against the recovered frontier. The observed eighty-second gap violates the sixty-second objective even if the operation completes quickly. The final incident report must record the violation rather than substitute a healthy replication-lag sample taken before the failure.

Recovery time objective, or RTO, is the target elapsed time to restore the agreed capability. Define the start as actual user-impact onset, not the moment an operator declares disaster. Suppose detection takes 45 seconds, decision and fencing take 75 seconds, replay takes 180 seconds, parallel dependency recovery takes 240 seconds, and canary validation plus routing takes 90 seconds. The serial critical-path total is 630 seconds, or 10.5 minutes. That leaves 270 seconds of a fifteen-minute budget for variance; it does not prove the tail will fit.

Failure variation: credentials for the standby expired, adding eight minutes. Observed recovery becomes 18.5 minutes and misses RTO. The production implication is to test the credentials and recovery path continuously, not merely speed up the database. The interview takeaway is to report RPO and RTO independently: a fast recovery can lose too much state, while a lossless recovery can take too long.

Availability and budgets use different denominators

Request availability is good eligible requests divided by all eligible requests. Time availability is good service time divided by measured service time. They answer different questions when traffic varies. A 99.95 percent time-based monthly target over thirty days permits 0.0005 times 43,200 minutes, or 21.6 minutes of unavailable time. A fifteen-minute regional recovery could consume most of that budget, while a partial overload outage consumes a request-based budget differently. Document exclusions and maintenance policy rather than hiding them in arithmetic. Google's availability appendix provides conventional time-based examples; the calculation here is an original application of that definition.

Chapter summary

Measure loss at a complete recovery frontier and time at the customer boundary. Budgets are assumptions to validate with distributions and drills, not guarantees created by adding optimistic averages.

03 · Fault domains and availability

Redundancy only helps across independent failures

Find the dependency that can invalidate every apparently redundant copy.

Reason about series and parallel paths

A request traverses an edge, identity validation, metadata lookup, model execution, and sometimes retrieval. If every component is required, the end-to-end success probability cannot exceed the weakest required component. Multiplying component availabilities is appropriate only under an independence model with compatible definitions and measurement periods. Operationally, dependencies often fail together. An identity outage can make both regions useless, and one bad configuration can invalidate every backend simultaneously.

For two independent replicas, each unavailable with probability q, the probability both are unavailable is q squared. That appealing equation leaves out failover coverage: detecting the failure, selecting the healthy copy, reaching it, and having sufficient capacity. Introduce a conditional coverage probability c for successful recovery when exactly one replica fails. In a simple snapshot model, service availability is (1−q)² + 2q(1−q)c. With q = 0.01 and c = 0.99, that is 0.9801 + 0.019602 = 0.999702. Perfect failover would give 0.9999. Imperfect recovery can therefore dominate the expected gain.

This is a teaching model, not a production forecast. It assumes identical independent replicas, one relevant fault state per observation, and no shared dependency failures. Introduce a separate common-cause outage probability r, and the approximation becomes (1−r) times the preceding result if that event is independent of replica-specific failures. The purpose is to reveal what must be measured. Assigning precise probabilities without evidence creates false confidence.

Design degraded service deliberately

A warm region with thirty percent of required GPU capacity is not a full-capacity recovery region. It can still provide a valuable contract: preserve high-priority traffic, serve a smaller approved model, disable expensive optional retrieval, or queue asynchronous work within a longer deadline. State these modes in advance. Silent substitution can violate output quality, residency, or customer model-pinning requirements. Some tenants may prefer a clear unavailable response to an unapproved model.

Separate dependencies that can fail open from those that must fail closed. An unavailable metrics export path should not necessarily reject inference. Unavailable authorization data may require rejecting operations unless a bounded, valid cached policy permits them. Bounded staleness needs an expiry rule and a revocation risk assessment. A cached entitlement is a capability with a lifetime, not a universal exception to security checks.

Deployment diversity is another fault-domain decision. Simultaneously updating both regions to the same untested binary defeats protection against software defects. Stage changes, retain a compatible previous model and schema, and keep recovery automation independent of the failed release's deployment controller where feasible. Independence costs operational effort, so prioritize the dependencies whose loss would otherwise invalidate recovery.

How I would explain this in an interview: “I draw the minimal set of services required for the degraded business path. Then I ask what single failure can remove all those paths. Replicas reduce independent failures; separate credentials, staged changes, and tested failover coverage address correlated ones. I will not multiply nines until the independence assumptions are defensible.” The stronger answer connects a reliability model to concrete engineering actions.

Chapter summary

Count independent failure boundaries, not deployed replicas. A recovery design must include routing, authorization, capacity, and operational coverage, with an explicit degraded-service contract.

04 · Replication and recovery frontiers

Know what an acknowledgment promises

A replica is useful only when its durability and publication rules match the business contract.

Synchronous and asynchronous boundaries

In asynchronous replication, the primary can acknowledge before the remote copy becomes durable. That reduces foreground latency and permits local progress through some inter-region failures, but exposes acknowledged state if the primary is lost. PostgreSQL's standby documentation explicitly describes this asynchronous loss window. A product-specific replication mode must be checked against its exact acknowledgment semantics; a phrase such as synchronous replication can refer to different remote persistence or application stages.

In a hypothetical synchronous design, a commit requires local log durability and remote durable confirmation before the response. Inter-region round-trip latency contributes to the critical path, though batching and overlap can change the throughput relationship. If the remote region is unavailable, either stop acknowledging writes or switch to a clearly declared weaker mode. Availability under partition and durability against immediate primary loss cannot both be preserved by pretending the remote acknowledgment happened.

The complete recovery frontier includes external objects. Suppose a database row publishes model version 42 before the associated weight objects are copied. The remote database is current but cannot serve that version. Use immutable objects, content hashes, a manifest listing every required object, and a publication record written only after the required region has verified availability. Garbage collection must respect retained manifests. Atomic publication of metadata is distinct from atomic transfer of every object.

Worked example 2: replication falls behind

Initial state: a change stream generates 120 MB per second. The replication path can deliver 150 MB per second, and a twenty-minute network interruption occurs. The objective is to restore a sixty-second RPO envelope without stopping normal traffic. At the end of the interruption, the backlog is 120 times 1,200, or 144,000 MB, using decimal units. Replication resumes, but current traffic consumes 120 MB per second of the 150 MB per second pipe. The net drain rate is only 30 MB per second.

Catch-up therefore takes 144,000 divided by 30, or 4,800 seconds: eighty minutes. Dividing backlog by the full 150 MB per second incorrectly predicts sixteen minutes because it ignores concurrent writes. The intermediate state is a shrinking backlog while both source and destination frontiers advance. The final state meets the normal lag objective only after sufficient excess bandwidth has drained the queue.

Failure variation: a failover occurs ten minutes into catch-up. The recovery point is determined by the durable complete frontier at that moment, not the nominal sixty-second target. If current generation rises to 160 MB per second, the backlog grows despite a healthy link. Alert on backlog age, growth rate, and remaining storage, and consider write admission, a larger link, or lower-priority data deferral. Every mitigation changes cost, latency, or service behavior.

Correctness requires retaining all needed log segments until the standby has safely advanced. A full primary disk caused by retention can turn a DR mechanism into a primary outage. Bound retention operationally and document the fallback to reseeding when retention limits are exceeded. The interview takeaway is that throughput margin determines recovery convergence. A redundant path that cannot catch up under peak load may never reenter its promised recovery envelope.

Backups protect a different axis

Replication preserves availability of recent state; versioned, independently protected backups enable recovery from logical corruption and deletion. Choose retention periods from detection delay as well as loss tolerance. If corruption is discovered after three days, a single latest snapshot can be useless. Restoring an older state may meet an integrity goal while exceeding ordinary RPO, so define a separate cyber-recovery or corruption-recovery objective. Keep keys, logs, and software needed to interpret the backup within the protection plan.

Chapter summary

Specify the acknowledgment boundary, publish only complete state, and provision replication headroom for catch-up. Recent replicas and historical backups address different failure classes.

05 · Ownership and failover protocol

Make promotion safe under uncertainty

Failure detection proposes a recovery; fencing makes the recovery safe.

A timeout is evidence, not proof

A controller in region B cannot distinguish a dead region A from a partition that isolates the controller while A still serves some customers. Increasing timeout sensitivity improves detection speed but increases false suspicions. Treat a failure detector as an input to a policy. It must not be the sole authority allowing conflicting writers. The correctness question is what prevents the old writer from committing after the new writer has been promoted.

One hypothetical protocol stores a monotonically increasing ownership epoch in a quorum-backed authority outside the failure being recovered. A transition from epoch 41 to 42 uses a conditional update against the expected prior revision. Every protected mutation must reach a resource that rejects tokens below its current accepted epoch, or the old writer must be demonstrably isolated by another reliable mechanism. A controller merely holding token 42 provides no protection if the database or external target ignores the token.

A third-region witness helps only if its protocol, placement, and resource enforcement actually establish exclusive authority. “Add a witness” is not a complete design. For a three-voter authority distributed one per region, any communicating two form a majority; one isolated voter cannot issue new authoritative transitions. That says nothing by itself about application writes already accepted by a partitioned primary. Connect the ownership decision to the write path and explain how delayed or in-flight operations are rejected.

Worked example 3: the old primary returns

Initial state: A owns protection group P under epoch 41. A request to attach volume V has a stable operation key K7, but its response is lost. B suspects A after several independent probes fail. The objective is to promote B without attaching V to conflicting compute instances or executing K7 twice. The recovery controller conditionally records a failover intent, obtains epoch 42, establishes resource-side fencing, and records evidence that the old owner can no longer commit protected mutations.

The intermediate states are suspected, authorized, fenced, recovering, validating, and active. Before replaying K7, B looks up the provider outcome using K7 or another stable external identifier. If the provider accepted it, B adopts that result. If the provider definitively rejected it, B can retry with the same identity under the documented provider contract. If the outcome remains unknown and duplication would be harmful, B pauses that branch for reconciliation instead of guessing.

A resumes with epoch 41 and tries to finish an old action. The protected resource rejects it as stale. That rejection is the concrete safety property; the operator's belief that A is dead is irrelevant. Final outcome: B becomes active only after authority, recovered state, and external effects have been reconciled. The recovery ledger records both the action and the evidence used to validate completion.

Failure variation: the external storage API cannot enforce epochs or deduplicate requests. The architecture must find a different boundary: revoke old credentials with verified propagation, isolate the old network path, use exclusive attachment semantics provided by storage, or retain manual recovery for that action. Each alternative needs its own failure analysis. The production implication is that exactly-once business effects require cooperation from effect owners. An orchestration log alone cannot make an arbitrary remote API transactional.

Failback is another ownership change

When A returns, do not automatically route writes back. Its state may contain divergent unreplicated changes. Preserve evidence, decide the authoritative history, rebuild or reconcile the returning replica, reverse replication safely, and run a planned switchover with its own readiness gates. Automatic oscillation between regions can multiply data loss and operator confusion. Use stabilization windows and explicit state transitions, but do not mistake a waiting period for a correctness proof.

Chapter summary

Detection concerns suspicion; fencing concerns authority. Safe promotion joins exclusive ownership, recoverable state, and reconciled external effects. Returning infrastructure must earn its way back into the topology.

06 · Orchestration and critical paths

Recover a dependency graph durably

Parallelize independent work without erasing ordering constraints or ambiguous outcomes.

From scripts to a persistent state machine

A recovery script that runs database promotion, storage attachment, model loading, and routing in sequence is easy to read but fragile under a controller crash. The successor must know which actions were intended, which were dispatched, which completed, and which are uncertain. Persist a plan version and step identities before dispatch. Store outcomes durably, then resume from observed evidence. A step's timeout is an unknown outcome unless the provider supplies a definitive rejection.

Represent prerequisites as a directed acyclic graph. A model-serving step might require network reachability, a valid model manifest, GPU capacity, and authorization configuration. A routing change requires successful canary validation. A topological scheduler can dispatch independent ready steps concurrently, while a per-protection-group execution lock or ownership epoch prevents conflicting plans. The graph describes ordering; it does not guarantee that each action is idempotent or that the graph matches reality.

Oracle's public documentation describes dependency-based recovery ordering and warns that modifying default plan order requires evaluation. That is a useful operational anchor for your existing knowledge. The durable journal, epoch protocol, and AI resource extensions taught here are hypothetical architecture choices, not undocumented claims about OCI Full Stack DR internals. Review a real service's supported lifecycle and API contract before adopting an example state machine.

Worked example 4: compute the recovery critical path

Initial state: detection and authorization are complete. Network restoration takes two minutes. Database replay takes four minutes and can start immediately. GPU allocation takes six minutes after network restoration. Model loading takes three minutes after both database replay and GPU allocation. Validation takes two minutes after model loading. The objective is to minimize time to a correct endpoint without admitting traffic early.

At time zero, network restoration and replay start in parallel. Network finishes at minute two, enabling GPU allocation through minute eight. Replay finishes at minute four. Model loading therefore begins at minute eight and ends at minute eleven. Validation ends at minute thirteen. The critical path is network, GPU allocation, model loading, validation: 2 + 6 + 3 + 2 = 13 minutes. Adding every duration predicts seventeen minutes and misses the benefit of concurrency. Assuming all steps run in parallel predicts six minutes and violates prerequisites.

Failure variation: GPU allocation times out at minute eight after the provider actually allocated the machines. A new allocation request might double resource consumption. Persist the original request key and query its result. If reconciliation takes two minutes, the model-load start shifts to minute ten and useful service returns at minute fifteen. The critical path changes because an uncertain external effect becomes a dependency.

Correctness reasoning follows the graph and each step's completion predicate. “GPU allocated” means the hosts are reachable with the correct accelerator type and security domain, not merely that a provisioning API returned accepted. “Model loaded” means checksum, runtime compatibility, and an inference health test succeeded. The final state opens traffic after validation. The production implication is to optimize the measured critical path and its tail, rather than accelerating a noncritical task because it is easy.

Design step contracts and cancellation

Each step needs an input schema, stable identity, precondition, postcondition, retry policy, timeout policy, and reconciliation procedure. Cancellation is a request to stop future work, not proof that remote work ceased. Some recovery effects cannot be undone safely. Rather than promising global rollback, define compensation where meaningful and a forward-recovery path for irreversible transitions. Persist which plan revision owns an effect so a later plan cannot accidentally adopt incompatible resources.

How I would explain this in an interview: “My recovery controller is a durable interpreter of a versioned dependency graph. It records intent before effects and validates postconditions after effects. Parallelism comes from independent prerequisites, while retries preserve action identity. I budget RTO using the critical path, including reconciliation of uncertain actions.”

Chapter summary

A graph provides concurrency; durable state provides resumability; action contracts provide safety. Recovery time is governed by the longest valid path, including validation and uncertain outcomes.

07 · Backups, restore, and integrity

Prove that the backup can become a service

A successful backup job is only the beginning of recovery evidence.

Protect the recovery chain

Consider an encrypted snapshot whose encryption key exists only in the failed region. Its storage durability does not provide recoverability. The same problem appears when a backup requires a missing database engine version, an unavailable schema migration, or credentials issued by a damaged identity service. Inventory the entire interpretation chain: snapshot, incremental bases, transaction logs, checksums, software, keys, policies, and operator access. A restore test should exercise this chain from a deliberately constrained environment.

Use independent administrative boundaries when the threat model includes compromised production credentials. Retention locks and offline or otherwise isolated copies can reduce deletion risk, but verify their operational behavior and governance. Avoid claiming that any storage setting protects against every administrator or legal deletion requirement. Security and recovery teams must agree who can authorize emergency access, how that access is audited, and how data-residency rules constrain recovery locations.

For application consistency, distinguish crash-consistent bytes from a business-consistent state. A database may recover its own transactional invariants while a separately copied object store lacks referenced documents. Publish consistent manifests or track a recoverable cross-system frontier. A restore that passes database integrity checks still needs business checks, such as matching accepted workflow IDs to execution records and verifying that a retrieval index contains only authorized documents.

Worked example 5: the bandwidth floor on restore time

Initial state: a service must restore a six-terabyte decimal snapshot to a clean region, then replay 900 GB of logs. The measured end-to-end snapshot transfer and materialization rate is 2 GB per second. Log replay proceeds at 0.5 GB per second after the snapshot is ready. The objective is recovery within sixty minutes. Ignore overlap initially to obtain a clearly stated baseline.

Snapshot time is 6,000 divided by 2, or 3,000 seconds: fifty minutes. Log replay time is 900 divided by 0.5, or 1,800 seconds: thirty minutes. The serial restore floor is eighty minutes before validation, provisioning, and routing. The design cannot meet sixty minutes under these assumptions. Compression may reduce transferred bytes but may shift the bottleneck to decompression or random writes. Quote measured effective throughput, not the network interface's advertised peak.

The intermediate states are clean environment provisioned, base snapshot verified, logs replayed to selected frontier, application invariants checked, then traffic admitted. To reduce time, compare prepositioned snapshots, more restore parallelism, partitioned restore, or serving a priority subset first. Each changes the restored capability. If a subset serves only premium tenants, do not report full recovery of every tenant.

Failure variation: a log segment near the selected frontier is corrupt. The restorer stops at the last verified contiguous segment rather than skipping a hole and calling the database current. The final recovery point moves backward, possibly violating RPO. Preserve the corrupt artifact for investigation and select an older verified chain. Production implication: restore throughput and chain integrity both constrain the usable recovery point. The interview takeaway is to use physics and integrity constraints early; orchestration cannot compress an eighty-minute serial data path into a sixty-minute promise.

Test the path without creating a second incident

Restore into an isolated environment with outbound effects disabled or redirected. A restored agent workflow must not replay real emails, purchases, or destructive cloud actions during validation. Sanitize or restrict production data according to policy. Validate counts, semantic invariants, authorization boundaries, representative queries, and the ability to rotate credentials. Then destroy the drill environment through an audited cleanup plan, retaining evidence and measurements rather than unnecessary sensitive copies.

Chapter summary

Recoverability is the ability to interpret, restore, validate, and safely expose protected state. Test bandwidth, log continuity, encryption access, and business invariants together.

08 · AI workload recovery

Recover inference, training, and agent effects differently

The valuable state is different in every AI workload.

Inference has a readiness and capacity problem

A model-serving process may need large immutable weights, a compatible tokenizer, quantization metadata, runtime binaries, policy configuration, and accelerator memory before it can serve. Recover these as a versioned deployment unit. A model hash without the matching tokenizer can produce incorrect behavior despite an otherwise healthy process. Measure recovery readiness using representative inference requests and latency at the promised degraded throughput.

For a simple capacity example, normal demand is 12,000 requests per second. The contracted emergency mode preserves 40 percent, or 4,800 requests per second. If a tested replica sustains 80 requests per second at the required latency and utilization ceiling for the specified request-length distribution, at least sixty replicas are needed before additional failure reserve. This number is not portable across models or token lengths. Include prompt length, output length, batching, and latency constraints in the benchmark. A queue that accepts everything while deadlines expire is not restored availability.

Routing twelve thousand requests per second into a standby designed for 4,800 creates overload and retry amplification. Apply admission control before redirecting traffic, reserve capacity by priority, and return explicit retriable errors with bounded backoff. Google's overload discussion supplies public grounding for capacity-aware overload handling; the replica counts and recovery policy here are original teaching assumptions.

Worked example 6: training checkpoint economics

Initial state: a hypothetical synchronized training job has a mean job interruption interval M of 360 minutes. A blocking consistent checkpoint costs C = 2 minutes, and a checkpoint is taken after every T minutes of useful computation. The objective is to choose an interval that balances checkpoint overhead and recomputation. Assume failures are rare relative to the interval, occur approximately uniformly within it, and all interruptions require restart from the last complete checkpoint.

The approximate lost fraction is C/T + T/(2M), excluding fixed restart overhead. The first term is checkpoint work per useful interval; the second is average lost work T/2 per interruption, scaled by interruption frequency 1/M. Differentiate with respect to T: −C/T² + 1/(2M). Setting this to zero gives T approximately equal to the square root of 2CM. Here that is the square root of 1,440, about 37.95 minutes. The two terms are each about 5.27 percent, totaling about 10.54 percent in this approximation.

The intermediate checkpoint state must not become the recovery frontier until every required rank's state and the manifest are complete. Include optimizer state, scheduler state, data position, and relevant randomness when reproducible continuation requires them. A pile of tensors with inconsistent training steps is not a valid checkpoint. Publication should identify the job attempt and reject stale attempts that finish after a newer attempt has taken ownership.

Failure variation: checkpoints overlap computation, failures are correlated, or recovery can restart only failed ranks. The simple formula no longer accurately models overhead; measure a workload-specific curve. Meta publicly discusses growing interruption risk with training scale, and archived NVIDIA NeMo documentation describes checkpoint-based resilience mechanisms. Neither source establishes the invented C and M values here. The production implication is to optimize useful training progress, not only process uptime. Interview takeaway: derive a simple model, then identify which assumptions production violates.

Agents have an external-effects problem

An agent can be restarted from a saved conversation yet repeat a harmful tool action. Preserve durable action identity and outcome evidence across recovery. Model-generated reasoning can be recomputed if the product allows it, but an already-executed payment cannot be made safe by regenerating the same prose. Anthropic's published distinction between workflows and agents establishes the tool-execution setting; this recovery protocol is an original design exercise. Recover authority and action ledgers before resuming effectful tools, even when read-only conversation rendering is already available.

Chapter summary

Inference recovery needs compatible artifacts and capacity. Training recovery needs consistent checkpoints and useful-progress accounting. Agent recovery needs durable action identity and reconciled business effects.

09 · Production architecture

Design an AI recovery control plane

A complete hypothetical system linking authority, protected data, and verified workload recovery.

Requirements, boundaries, and data model

Design a service that recovers tenant inference deployments and durable agent workflows across two workload regions. Assume ten thousand protection groups, a third authority location, a fifteen-minute regional RTO for priority workloads, a sixty-second RPO for ordinary asynchronous workflow metadata, and a zero-loss class that requires cross-region durable acknowledgment. These are proposed contracts to negotiate, not a single universal promise. Destructive actions require exclusive authority, and all recovered data must remain within tenant-approved locations.

The control plane manages plans, readiness, ownership, and audit. The data plane serves inference and executes permitted tools from cached, valid deployment configuration. Keep already-running safe workloads operating through a temporary management outage. A durable authority stores the current epoch and active execution per protection group. A recovery journal records immutable intent and step outcomes. Regional adapters call provider APIs and report observed state. Artifact storage holds immutable versioned manifests and model objects. A readiness service continuously measures recoverable frontiers, credentials, quota, and standby capacity.

Expose a create-execution API with protection-group ID, expected plan revision, operation type, idempotency key, and requested recovery class. Return an execution ID and a status resource. A repeated key with the same canonical request returns the original execution; the same key with different parameters is rejected. Authorize against tenant and operation scope before allocating an execution. Status must distinguish accepted, waiting for authority, recovering, validating, complete, and needs intervention. “Accepted” must never be rendered as “recovered.”

Persist protection-group desired configuration, versioned plan DAGs, execution epochs, step attempts, provider operation IDs, selected recovery frontier, evidence digests, and audit actors. Partition ordinary execution records by tenant and protection group, with a separate index for operational queries. A compare-and-set guards the single active execution invariant. Transactionally create the execution intent and ownership reference within the chosen metadata boundary. Cross-group recovery needs a separate coordination contract; do not assume per-group transactions become globally atomic.

Normal execution and regional failure

In normal operation, readiness probes verify replication and resources without modifying production. A planned switchover quiesces new effects, drains or reconciles in-flight actions, catches up the remote frontier, changes ownership, validates the standby, and redirects traffic. An unplanned failover cannot rely on graceful cooperation from the primary. It must establish fencing independently, select the best verified recovery frontier, disclose expected loss, and apply the approved policy before resuming.

When the recovery controller crashes after dispatching a provider action, its successor reads the journal and queries the provider rather than issuing an unrelated replacement. If the metadata authority loses quorum, it cannot grant new ownership. Existing data-plane operations may continue only within their existing authority and policy bounds. If all ownership proof is unavailable, effectful operations may stop while read-only cached inference remains possible under the contract. This is a deliberate availability decision at a safety boundary.

For a ten-thousand-group disaster, issuing every plan simultaneously can overwhelm IAM, compute quotas, storage APIs, and the journal. Suppose each plan has twenty provider actions: that is 200,000 actions before retries. At a safe aggregate provider budget of 250 actions per second, dispatch alone requires at least 800 seconds if every action must pass that bottleneck. Prioritize critical groups, reserve budgets for reconciliation, apply tenant fairness, and separate read probes from mutation budgets. The fifteen-minute objective cannot be promised for every group without further capacity evidence.

Security, operations, alternatives, and evolution

Use scoped service identities, short-lived credentials where supported, audit trails outside the affected administrative boundary, and explicit approval for high-risk loss acceptance. Encrypt data and recover keys under an independently tested process. Treat imported plans and model-generated suggestions as untrusted input. Validate resource references, tenant ownership, graph acyclicity, and permitted action types. An agent may propose a plan; deterministic policy and authorized operators decide which destructive effects can run.

Observe time to detect, fence, restore, validate, and admit traffic; record each stage separately. Track recoverable-frontier age, unknown-effect count, stale-token rejections, standby capacity by workload class, and drill success. Page on customer symptoms and imminent safety or loss violations, while retaining detailed component telemetry for diagnosis. A runbook should name incident command, authority evidence, loss-acceptance policy, abort points, customer communications, and the failback procedure.

Start with a small set of explicit recovery classes and a single-region journal plus a tested recovery copy if requirements permit. Evolve toward a multi-region authority and partitioned journals when measured RTO and management availability justify the complexity. Active-active execution is an alternative only with ownership and conflict semantics per resource. Do not deploy it solely to avoid saying “standby.” Defend the simplest topology that actually meets the agreed failure model, then identify the signal that would force the next architectural step.

Chapter summary

The recovery control plane coordinates authority, state, effects, and capacity. Its own recovery path must not depend exclusively on the services it is trying to restore.

10 · Validation and engineering judgment

Turn recovery claims into evidence

Use executable models and progressively stronger drills without confusing simulation with proof.

A small executable capacity model

The following Python example computes two things the worked examples require: backlog catch-up time and the earliest finish time of a recovery DAG. It is an executable teaching model using only the standard library. It does not call cloud APIs, implement fencing, or simulate real network distributions. Its value is making assumptions explicit and catching arithmetic or dependency errors before an operational promise is made.

The catch-up function subtracts continuing input from transfer capacity. If capacity is no larger than input and backlog is positive, the backlog cannot drain under the fixed-rate model. Zero backlog returns zero even if the rates are equal. The scheduling function resolves a step only after all its dependencies have finished. It adds the step duration to the largest prerequisite finish time. When no pending step can be resolved, the input has a cycle or a missing prerequisite; silently returning an optimistic schedule would hide a plan defect.

Every duration is a nonnegative number in minutes, and every rate uses the same decimal byte units. Floating-point arithmetic is sufficient for this illustrative estimate; a production scheduler should use a well-defined duration representation, explicit deadlines, and measured distributions. The algorithm scans pending steps repeatedly and has quadratic worst-case work in the number of steps plus dependency traversal. A queue-based topological algorithm can achieve linear time in vertices plus edges. This small implementation favors readable validation over scale.

PYTHON · executable standard-library model
from math import inf, sqrt

def catchup(backlog, incoming, capacity):
    if min(backlog, incoming, capacity) < 0:
        raise ValueError('negative input')
    if backlog == 0:
        return 0.0
    return backlog / (capacity - incoming) if capacity > incoming else inf

def finish_times(steps):
    # name -> (nonnegative minutes, prerequisite names)
    if any(duration < 0 for duration, _ in steps.values()):
        raise ValueError('negative duration')
    done = {}
    pending = dict(steps)
    while pending:
        ready = [name for name, (_, deps) in pending.items()
                 if all(dep in done for dep in deps)]
        if not ready:
            raise ValueError('cycle or missing prerequisite')
        for name in ready:
            duration, deps = pending.pop(name)
            done[name] = duration + max((done[d] for d in deps), default=0)
    return done

steps = {
    'network': (2, []),
    'replay': (4, []),
    'gpu': (6, ['network']),
    'load': (3, ['gpu', 'replay']),
    'validate': (2, ['load']),
}
assert catchup(144_000, 120, 150) == 4_800
assert catchup(10, 150, 150) == inf
assert catchup(0, 150, 150) == 0
assert finish_times(steps)['validate'] == 13
try:
    finish_times({'a': (1, ['b']), 'b': (1, ['a'])})
except ValueError:
    pass
else:
    raise AssertionError('cycle accepted')
assert round(sqrt(2 * 2 * 360), 2) == 37.95
print('catch-up: 80 minutes; recovery: 13 minutes; model tests passed')

The assertions reproduce the eighty-minute catch-up and thirteen-minute critical path from the earlier examples. An additional cycle test verifies rejection. To extend the exercise, add a two-minute reconciliation dependency before model loading and show why the finish time increases. Then model a provider concurrency limit: the pure DAG lower bound is no longer a complete schedule because otherwise independent steps compete for a finite resource.

Drills, distributions, and cost

Begin with plan linting and restore checks, then isolate a component, then a zone, then the regional dependency set under controlled conditions. Each stage answers a different question. A tabletop drill tests decisions and communications; it cannot establish measured storage throughput. A successful restore tests data access; it cannot establish that routing and authorization recover during an identity outage. Record the environment, workload, disabled dependencies, and exceptions alongside each result.

Do not obtain a tail RTO estimate by summing independently measured p99 stage times and calling the result the end-to-end p99. Stages can be correlated, and quantiles do not add that way. Measure joint end-to-end runs, preserve per-stage timings for diagnosis, and use scenario analysis when sample counts are too small for a stable tail estimate. Rare regional events make statistical confidence difficult; engineering evidence includes drills and dependency proofs as well as incident history.

Compare costs at the business-contract level. A warm standby spends on idle replicas, replicated storage, bandwidth, testing, and operational complexity. A cold restore saves idle compute but adds provisioning and data movement to RTO. A smaller emergency model can reduce GPU reserve while changing customer capability. Calculate expected loss only if failure probability and impact estimates are defensible, and include irrecoverable regulatory or trust consequences that a simple hourly-revenue model misses.

A useful decision record states the objective, options rejected, evidence, assumptions, owner, and review trigger. For example: choose warm priority inference because measured cold loading exceeds the fifteen-minute target; revisit when model size doubles or standby utilization exceeds the tested emergency ceiling. That is Senior Staff judgment: tie architecture to evidence and make the conditions for changing the decision legible to the next team.

Chapter summary

Executable calculations reveal impossible budgets. Drills reveal hidden dependencies. Neither substitutes for stating the failure model, observing real outcomes, and keeping assumptions current.

11 · Original Senior Staff interview practice

Defend recovery under changing requirements

These are original practice questions, not reported questions from any company.

Twelve architecture questions

1. The endpoint is back in four minutes. Did we meet RTO?

Evaluation: business-boundary reasoning. First establish the promised capability and when user impact began. If only health checks work while authorization or retrieval is broken, recovery is incomplete. Measure a representative authorized operation, its latency, and the promised throughput. A degraded contract can count only if it was explicitly agreed. Report component restoration and business restoration separately.

Follow-up: what if premium traffic works but all ordinary traffic is rejected? Define per-class objectives and denominators. Common mistake: resetting the clock at disaster declaration. Tradeoff: granular objectives improve honesty but need clear product communication and operational ownership.

2. Can you promise zero RPO and uninterrupted writes through a two-region partition?

Evaluation: acknowledgment semantics and failure assumptions. If either region can be immediately destroyed, acknowledging writes on both isolated sides without a coordination or conflict contract cannot preserve a single lossless authoritative history. Require remote durable acknowledgment and accept write unavailability when that path fails, or negotiate weaker durability or explicit conflict semantics. Name the exact class of protected acknowledgments.

Follow-up: can a third witness replace a data copy? A witness may establish authority but cannot reconstruct bytes it never stored. Common mistake: treating consensus and data replication as interchangeable. Tradeoff: stronger regional durability adds latency and may reduce partition-time write availability.

3. The replication link is healthy, but lag keeps growing. What do you do?

Evaluation: queue stability. Compare generated bytes per second against sustained delivered and applied bytes per second, including replay and storage bottlenecks. A positive service margin is necessary for catch-up. Estimate backlog divided by net drain, monitor age and retention headroom, and prioritize or throttle inputs under an approved policy. A link-up metric alone says little about recovery readiness.

Follow-up: what happens when retained logs fill the primary disk? Define alerts, bounded retention, and reseeding behavior. Common mistake: using full link bandwidth as catch-up bandwidth. Tradeoff: more capacity costs money; write throttling protects recoverability at the expense of foreground throughput.

4. Both regions think they own the database. Where is safety enforced?

Evaluation: concrete fencing. Identify the authority issuing ownership and the resource rejecting stale ownership. A monotonically increasing epoch is useful only if the protected write path checks it atomically with the effect, or another reliable isolation boundary prevents old writes. Trace delayed and in-flight requests. If the database cannot enforce the protocol, use its supported promotion and exclusion mechanisms rather than inventing a header.

Follow-up: the old process pauses for ten minutes and resumes. Show rejection after resume. Common mistake: assuming process health or a lease alone stops external writes. Tradeoff: fail-closed fencing can lengthen recovery but prevents divergent histories.

5. The controller crashed after a cloud API timed out. Is retry safe?

Evaluation: uncertain effects. Persist a stable action identity before dispatch. Query by provider operation ID or replay the same idempotency key under its documented retention and parameter rules. A timeout means unknown, not failed. If duplication is harmful and the provider supplies neither deduplication nor lookup, pause and reconcile through an alternative source of truth. Do not claim exactly-once effects from the controller log alone.

Follow-up: the provider's deduplication window expired. Explain the new uncertainty. Common mistake: generating a fresh key on each retry. Tradeoff: refusing ambiguous work preserves safety while consuming recovery time and operator effort.

6. All backups pass checksums, but none can be restored. What did the design miss?

Evaluation: recovery-chain completeness. Checksums establish byte integrity relative to a stored digest, not decryptability, software compatibility, log continuity, or business consistency. Inventory keys, identities, incremental bases, versions, and object references. Restore into a clean restricted environment and execute business validation. Protect recovery access from the same administrative compromise as production.

Follow-up: corruption was detected three days later. Choose an older verified chain and disclose the loss. Common mistake: using backup-job success as the recovery SLI. Tradeoff: longer retention and independent administration cost more but cover delayed discovery and credential compromise.

7. A fifteen-minute plan takes forty minutes during a regional outage. Why?

Evaluation: correlated bottlenecks. Reconstruct detection-to-validation timing, then compare the drill's assumptions with actual quota contention, API throttling, credential dependencies, data volume, and standby capacity. A small drill may not exercise a ten-thousand-group recovery storm. Bound global mutation concurrency, reserve reconciliation capacity, and prioritize groups by explicit recovery class. Do not hide queueing time outside the RTO clock.

Follow-up: which investment most reduces the critical path? Use measured stage contributions and tail correlation. Common mistake: speeding up a noncritical task. Tradeoff: reserved capacity improves worst-case recovery but increases steady-state expense.

8. Does a warm inference standby need every normal feature?

Evaluation: product and capacity negotiation. Define emergency capability by tenant class, model version, latency, throughput, and required tools. Optional features may be disabled only under a documented contract. Preload the expensive critical path, retain compatible artifacts, and test with realistic token distributions. Apply admission control before traffic shift to avoid turning partial recovery into overload.

Follow-up: can a smaller model answer regulated workloads? Check approval and pinning rather than assuming interchangeability. Common mistake: treating any generated response as successful recovery. Tradeoff: narrower emergency capability reduces reserve cost but may not satisfy every customer.

9. How often should a large training job checkpoint?

Evaluation: modeling and limitations. Start with blocking cost C and mean interruption interval M. Under rare uniform failures, minimize C/T + T/(2M), yielding approximately sqrt(2CM). Then challenge blocking behavior, failure correlation, fixed restart time, and checkpoint consistency. Measure useful progress per accelerator-hour rather than choosing an interval from habit. Publish only complete attempt-consistent manifests.

Follow-up: asynchronous checkpointing overlaps compute but consumes bandwidth. Model the contention instead of setting C to zero. Common mistake: using single-device reliability as whole-job reliability. Tradeoff: frequent checkpoints reduce rework while increasing I/O and synchronization overhead.

10. How do you prove a recovery plan will not leak tenant data?

Evaluation: isolation throughout recovery. Authorize the initiator and service identities, validate every resource reference, enforce approved target regions, recover keys with scope, and preserve document-level policy in restored retrieval data. Run cross-tenant negative tests. Recovery shortcuts must not introduce an unrestricted service credential. Audit effectful actions independently of ordinary application logs.

Follow-up: a drill restores production data into a developer account. Reject that plan unless approved controls make the environment appropriate. Common mistake: treating emergencies as an authorization bypass. Tradeoff: emergency access must be usable, but bounded, tested, and accountable.

11. Can we automatically fail back as soon as the old region is healthy?

Evaluation: divergent state and stability. Health does not establish currency or authority. Preserve old state, select the authoritative lineage, reconcile or reseed, verify reverse replication, and conduct a planned ownership transition. Define stabilization policy and prevent concurrent failback plans. If failback introduces more risk than temporary operation in the recovery region, defer it deliberately.

Follow-up: the returning primary contains acknowledged writes absent from the standby. Escalate a data-reconciliation decision rather than overwriting evidence. Common mistake: DNS-based oscillation. Tradeoff: remaining in the recovery region may cost more but avoids an unnecessary second disruption.

12. Leadership wants five nines and no standby budget. How do you respond?

Evaluation: architectural leadership. Clarify the measurement, failure scope, loss tolerance, and impact. Quantify available error budget, restore floors, and capacity gaps. Offer concrete alternatives: a lower objective, a narrower emergency capability, reserved priority capacity, or additional investment. Record assumptions and an accountable decision. Do not present an impossible commitment as an engineering optimization exercise.

Follow-up: the business accepts downtime but no data loss. Shift investment toward durable copies and tested restore instead of idle compute. Common mistake: arguing about technologies before agreeing on objectives. Tradeoff: reliability spending should purchase a specified business capability with evidence.

Architecture defense: the witness is unreachable

The interviewer removes the third authority location during a regional partition and demands immediate write recovery. Clarify which voters remain connected, what authority the old region retains, whether the datastore supports exclusion, and whether acknowledged loss is acceptable. Do not infer that an unreachable authority has approved promotion. A strong model solution keeps existing safe reads available, stops new conflicting effects without ownership proof, and offers an explicitly authorized alternative isolation procedure if one is independently verifiable. If no such procedure exists, report the recovery objective at risk rather than violate the single-writer invariant. Then propose reducing this correlated dependency through authority placement or a different supported data architecture.

Chapter summary

A strong answer names the guarantee, traces a failure, quantifies a bottleneck, and defends the business tradeoff. Memorized component lists cannot substitute for these steps.

12 · Speaking and recall

Explain the architecture at three depths

Practice a coherent argument, then adapt it when an assumption changes.

Thirty-second explanation

High availability keeps a defined service useful through expected faults. Disaster recovery restores that capability after a larger failure. RPO limits acceptable lost state; RTO limits restoration time. I start with the business path, then design durable state, exclusive ownership, dependency-aware recovery, and validation. The proof is a realistic drill, including capacity and security.

Two-minute explanation

I would begin by asking what customers must be able to do after the failure, which failures we cover, and which acknowledged operations must survive. An inference endpoint, an agent action ledger, and a deployment console have different recovery needs. I would define per-capability RPO and RTO, including the degraded throughput that counts as restored service.

Then I would trace the data and authority boundaries. Asynchronous replication exposes recent acknowledged writes; synchronous cross-region durability changes latency and partition behavior. Promotion must prevent the old owner from writing, not merely suspect that it is dead. A recovery journal records intent before external actions and reconciles unknown outcomes after crashes.

I would recover the stack as a dependency graph and budget the critical path through fencing, replay, GPU readiness, model loading, validation, and routing. Standby capacity and provider API limits are part of that budget. Backups cover corruption and deletion only if their keys, log chains, and software are recoverable. Finally, I would test the full customer path under the named failure and record actual loss and elapsed time. I would defend the simplest topology supported by those measurements, with explicit triggers for revisiting it.

Five-minute explanation

Suppose we are recovering an enterprise AI platform after losing a workload region. Before drawing components, I would separate capabilities: reading conversations, producing inference, performing effectful tools, and managing deployments. I would ask which tenants receive emergency capacity, what latency counts as success, which regions are permitted, and whether model substitution is allowed. Those answers determine the recovery contract.

For state, I would identify an authoritative complete frontier. A replicated database position is insufficient when it references model objects or documents that are absent remotely. Immutable objects and verified manifests make publication explicit. Ordinary metadata might use asynchronous replication with a sixty-second objective. A zero-loss class would require an acknowledgment rule that actually includes remote durable state. If that path fails, we must stop those acknowledgments or clearly change the contract.

For ownership, I would distinguish a failure detector from a fencing mechanism. A timeout cannot prove the primary stopped. A quorum-backed epoch can authorize a successor, but the protected resource must reject stale epochs or the old owner must be reliably excluded by a supported mechanism. When that proof is unavailable, effectful writes may remain unavailable. That is an intentional safety choice, not an exception hidden in the runbook.

The recovery workflow would persist a versioned plan and stable action identities before dispatch. A controller crash after a provider timeout leaves an uncertain outcome. The successor queries or safely replays the same identity; it does not create a new action and hope. Independent steps run concurrently through a dependency graph. Routing waits for semantic validation, and cancellation does not pretend to undo completed external effects.

I would quantify the critical path. Data transfer, replay, accelerator allocation, model loading, and validation each impose a floor. Ten thousand recovery plans can saturate provider APIs even when one plan is fast, so I would bound concurrency, prioritize recovery classes, and reserve reconciliation capacity. At the serving boundary, admission control prevents redirected demand from overwhelming the smaller standby. A successful health check does not establish usable emergency throughput.

For integrity, I would keep independently protected historical recovery chains and exercise them in a clean environment with side effects disabled. Encryption keys, schema compatibility, log continuity, and tenant authorization are part of the restore. Training checkpoints and agent actions need distinct validation: consistent optimizer and data state for training, durable effect identity for agents. I would track useful training progress and safe business completion rather than process uptime alone.

Finally, I would measure time from customer impact through restored capability, report observed loss separately, and preserve stage timings and evidence. Drills would progressively remove dependencies and test the same failure boundary we promise. I would record alternatives and the conditions that invalidate my recommendation. The architecture is defensible when its guarantees, assumptions, recovery evidence, and cost all refer to the same business contract.

Important concepts and final interview cheat sheet

  • Capability boundary: define what successful recovery enables.
  • Failure domain: enumerate shared causes, including administration.
  • RPO: bound lost committed business state.
  • RTO: bound impact-to-restoration time.
  • Recovery frontier: identify complete, usable state.
  • Acknowledgment rule: specify the durability actually promised.
  • Fencing: reject stale owners at the effect boundary.
  • Unknown outcome: reconcile instead of assuming failure.
  • Critical path: respect dependencies and constrained resources.
  • Catch-up margin: subtract ongoing writes from replication service.
  • Restore chain: include keys, versions, logs, and integrity.
  • Degraded capacity: admit only the tested emergency workload.
  • Recovery evidence: preserve drill scope and observed outcomes.
  • Failback: validate lineage before returning authority.

Common interview mistakes are substituting replicas for independence, routing for recovery, backup completion for restore evidence, or a timeout for proof of failure. Replace each shortcut with an invariant, a concrete sequence of events, and a measurement. Useful language includes “under these assumptions,” “the acknowledgment boundary is,” and “this protects against this fault but not that one.” These phrases make the scope of a guarantee precise.

Whiteboard and speaking exercises

In thirty seconds, define RPO and RTO without using the word uptime. In two minutes, explain why 150 MB per second of replication cannot instantly clear a backlog while 120 MB per second of new writes continue. In five minutes, defend the hypothetical control plane while the interviewer removes its authority location. On a whiteboard, draw ownership, replication, external effects, and recovery dependencies as separate boundaries. Then inject one stale writer, one corrupt log segment, and one exhausted quota. State the invariant and the customer-visible consequence at every step.

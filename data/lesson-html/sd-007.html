SD-007 · Distributed Systems Foundations · 01

What a timeout leaves unknown

A recovery worker asks a database provider to promote a standby. The provider may have accepted the request just before the worker lost power. Its successor must decide what to do before the primary recovers and begins taking traffic again.

Worked example 1 · Two indistinguishable histories

Begin with one healthy standby S, no recorded promotion operation, and a workflow that says promotion is the next step. In history A the worker builds a request, crashes before transmitting it, and leaves the workflow unchanged. In history B the provider receives the request, begins promotion, and sends operation number 617 just as the worker crashes. The workflow again looks unchanged. On restart the successor sees precisely the same local evidence. Its next instruction could send a request, wait for a callback, or ask a human. No amount of local computation can distinguish A from B.

Suppose the successor always sends another request. That improves progress in A. In B it might start a second promotion, depending on whether the provider recognizes a duplicate or imposes a sufficiently strong resource constraint. Suppose instead that the successor always skips the step. It avoids a duplicate in B, but in A the recovery controller may publish traffic to a standby that never became writable. The problem is an information gap between two independently durable systems. Saving an operation number after the call helps only when the process survives long enough to perform that save.

Change the order of local work. The workflow first commits an intent with an identity that the successor can reconstruct without the provider's response. Let the identity be K and its target be standby S at recovery generation 19. The provider accepts a caller-specified key scoped to this tenant and the promote operation, binds it to the arguments, and durably associates that key with at most one operation. If K is submitted again within the agreed horizon, the caller can obtain operation 617 or an equivalent durable reference. Now the same recovery action handles both histories: resubmit the original intent and follow the resulting operation to its terminal outcome. The provider contract and the caller's write ordering jointly make this safe.

One more distinction matters to an on-call engineer. An accepted operation can still fail after it has been deduplicated successfully. Retrying the same intent may correctly return the same failed operation. The policy for requesting another actual promotion belongs to the workflow's business state machine. It should read the failure, recheck the resource's current generation, and record a deliberate new intent if the class of failure permits another attempt. Automatically replacing K after any failed response collapses this distinction and can recreate the original ambiguity.

Define the observable effect

For a mathematical function, idempotence means applying it twice produces the same value as applying it once. For a service, first name the state or observations over which that comparison is made. Setting a routing weight to 25 may leave the same final configuration after two applications. If both calls trigger a paid rollout, send two notifications and increment a customer-visible deployment counter twice, the combined operation has a different observable history. A design can promise convergence of routing state, one billed deployment, or one notification. Each promise needs its own enforcement boundary.

Define a logical intent as the caller's decision to perform a business action once. A transport attempt is one transmission of that intent. An execution is a handler's effort to process it. A committed effect is the change an authorized observer can rely on. Five transport attempts can result in three handler executions, two speculative computations and one committed debit. Conversely one request received by an API can fan out into several downstream attempts. A phrase such as exactly once is meaningful only after attaching it to an effect, a scope, a retention interval and failure assumptions. In a design review I would ask the speaker to complete the sentence with those four details.

How I would explain this in an interview

I would start by drawing the two possible crash histories and asking what evidence survives in both. Before dispatch I persist the identity of the intended action and the parameters that define it. At the mutation boundary I need an atomic association between that identity and the effect or an equivalent condition that prevents a second effect. If I cannot obtain that property, I expose an uncertain outcome and reconcile before taking a conflicting step. I separately measure time to customer recovery because safely detecting duplicate work does not guarantee that the original action finishes.

Design an identity that means what the caller meant

A hash of identical request bodies can merge two independent purchases. A new random UUID on every retry can turn one purchase into several. Correct identification starts by deciding who is entitled to decide whether two actions are the same.

Worked example 2 · Two equal reservations and one replay

An operator requests four GPUs for training run A and later four more for a separate run B. The selected pool, requested size and machine class are identical. A body hash that excludes the run identity can incorrectly collapse B into A. A mechanism that hashes the entire network request has the opposite failure: a tracing header, JSON field order or regenerated timestamp can make the retry of A appear new. In both cases the service delegated a semantic decision to incidental serialization. Equal quantities are weak evidence about the caller's intent.

Instead the client's workflow creates key K1 for the first reservation and K2 for the second. The authority uses tenant, operation family and caller key as the uniqueness scope. For each unique key it also stores the immutable request fields or their canonical fingerprint, the schema version used for normalization, the resulting operation ID and enough outcome information for recovery. K1 arriving again with four requested GPUs returns its original result. K1 arriving with eight raises a deterministic conflict. K2 with the same four-unit body is eligible for a fresh reservation. The primary key expresses identity; the fingerprint detects accidental or incompatible reuse of that identity.

Describe the canonicalization policy with examples. If an omitted zone means choose any zone, converting omission to today's default zone can change the meaning of a retry tomorrow. Capture the effective parameters or retain the original argument representation with its versioned interpretation. Preserve list order for ordered constraints and normalize order only for genuinely unordered sets. Specify floating-point limits, units and null semantics before hashing them. A canonicalization change first deployed to half the fleet must not make two handlers disagree about the same request. Version the normalization format and retain an interpretation for records still within their replay window.

A scoped key is also an access-control boundary. Replaying a response can disclose another tenant's operation or sensitive output if keys are looked up globally before verifying authorization. Authenticate the current caller and check access to the operation even on the duplicate path. A stored response containing a signed download URL might be unusable after the credential expires. Record the immutable artifact identity and operation outcome. Return a fresh authorized link where the API promises semantic equivalence, or separate the status endpoint from signed URL issuance. Returning the same meaning does not always mean returning the exact old wire bytes.

Choose identities at every hop

Use different identifiers for questions that have different lifetimes. A trace identifier can change on every HTTP attempt because its job is to help trace one path through the system. A provider operation ID becomes available after acceptance, while a caller-generated key has to exist before acceptance. A workflow run ID alone can be too broad because one recovery plan may legitimately promote many resources. A tuple of run incarnation, stable logical step identity and target resource is often a better starting point. Include a new action generation for a deliberate second intent. Exclude transient worker, attempt count and elected owner.

Fan-out needs a durable mapping between the parent intent and its child intents. Suppose a plan fans out to ten storage volumes. A new inventory enumeration after a crash can return the same resources in a new order. A child key derived from loop index five can then refer to a different volume. Persist the selected resource membership and derive identifiers from stable resource identities, or persist the complete child-command set transactionally. If a new volume becomes eligible during recovery, apply an explicit membership change policy. Letting a changed enumeration silently reuse an existing key produces difficult conflicts that appear only during faults.

A speaking drill on identity

Tell the interviewer which party decides two requests represent the same action. Then give two counterexamples to using a body hash, one where it merges distinct actions and one where it splits a retry. Finish by saying how a user can legitimately request a second action. A usable interface gives clients a way to make that decision deliberately and makes conflicting reuse observable. A clean abstraction is one that still lets an operator distinguish a user requesting another recovery exercise from a worker retransmitting yesterday's lost response.

SD-007 · Distributed Systems Foundations · 03

Make the decision and the effect atomic

A duplicate-suppression table can prevent concurrent work, lose accepted work, or faithfully remember an incorrect result. Its behavior depends on what transaction it shares with the actual business mutation.

Worked example 3 · The quota ledger race

Tenant T has ten units of free quota. Workers A and B receive the same six-unit reservation K simultaneously after a proxy retries a slow call. Both first query a deduplication table and see no record. Both read ten units of quota. If their later writes are independently committed, both can send a success receipt. Even if a lost-update anomaly leaves the quota counter at four, the system may have issued two distinct six-unit entitlements. Reconstruct the invariant over actual reservation records and entitlement IDs; a reassuring aggregate counter can hide an over-allocation.

In a single transactional store, use a unique constraint on the scoped key and a transaction that includes the request binding, reservation, quota transition and stable outcome. Give the reservation its own unique identity and enforce a conditional capacity check under appropriate isolation or locking. A concurrent insert may wait, lose the unique constraint race or require a retry under the database's conflict semantics. The losing transaction must load the committed winner in a valid transaction snapshot and compare its fingerprint before returning its receipt. A unique index alone does not make read-modify-write quota arithmetic safe between distinct keys.

Review the crash points. Before transaction commit there is no accepted business outcome, assuming the database rolls the incomplete transaction back. After commit the quota debit, reservation and request record survive together under the promised durability model. A client timeout during commit is still ambiguous to the caller, but reissuing K will discover the committed outcome or perform the transition once. If the debit is committed in database A and the request record is sent to cache B, a crash between the writes permits a second debit. Moving the cache update earlier can suppress a debit that never happened. No ordering of two independent writes cures both failure histories.

The executable Python model below keeps a single transaction domain and uses a stable primary key. Its transaction either persists both changes or neither. An assertion exercises conflicting payloads and another exercises rejected requests. It intentionally models a single connection and local transaction semantics. To qualify a real service, test concurrent independent connections against the actual isolation level, unique-conflict handling, commit response loss and election behavior. Success in an in-memory demonstration proves only the demonstrated state transitions.

Durable admission for asynchronous work

A slow operation often cannot hold the database transaction open until a GPU has actually started. An effective contract accepts work in one transaction and completes it later through a durable operation resource. The client receives an operation ID or a replay of that ID. The accepted record contains its immutable arguments and durable ownership of remaining work, commonly in an outbox or a polled operations table. A worker can be claimed with a lease to avoid waste. A lease expiry means the next worker can try to recover the operation; it says nothing by itself about which external effects the old worker already performed.

Choose which failures become stable results. Malformed requests may be rejected before an intent is admitted. A deterministic business rejection such as an exhausted one-time voucher can be recorded if the API defines the rejection as the final outcome of that intent. A failure to contact the database before the admission decision generally cannot be stored as an authoritative outcome by that very database. Treat storage errors and conflicts according to their actual contracts. A replayable failure may surprise a caller that expects retrying the same key to reevaluate newer capacity. Document how to create a new business attempt and what makes doing so safe.

Statuses need meaningful recovery semantics. A pending row can be proof of durable admission, a placeholder inserted before dispatch, or a stranded record abandoned by a crashed handler. Only the first meaning supplies recoverable progress if the system actually schedules and monitors the remaining work. A distinct status for external outcome unknown can block further conflicting dispatch while authorizing safe lookup. A periodic reconciler finds overdue operations, reuses the same effect identity and updates the status from authoritative evidence. Add an age alert for the oldest uncertain operation. An API that always returns the same pending status is idempotent in a narrow sense yet completely unusable.

PYTHON · executable model
import sqlite3

db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
db.executescript("""
CREATE TABLE quota (remaining INTEGER NOT NULL);
INSERT INTO quota VALUES (10);
CREATE TABLE requests (
    key TEXT PRIMARY KEY, units INTEGER NOT NULL, result TEXT NOT NULL
);
""")

def reserve(key, units):
    if units <= 0:
        raise ValueError("units must be positive")
    with db:
        # Serialize the decision in this SQLite model.
        db.execute("BEGIN IMMEDIATE")
        old = db.execute("SELECT units, result FROM requests WHERE key=?",
                         (key,)).fetchone()
        if old is not None:
            if old[0] != units:
                raise ValueError("key reused with different units")
            return old[1]
        updated = db.execute(
            "UPDATE quota SET remaining=remaining-? WHERE remaining>=?",
            (units, units))
        result = "reserved" if updated.rowcount == 1 else "insufficient"
        db.execute("INSERT INTO requests VALUES (?, ?, ?)", (key, units, result))
        return result

assert reserve("K", 6) == reserve("K", 6) == "reserved"
assert reserve("L", 6) == "insufficient"
assert db.execute("SELECT remaining FROM quota").fetchone() == (4,)
try:
    reserve("K", 8)
except ValueError:
    pass
else:
    raise AssertionError("changed intent was accepted")

Recover an external call that cannot share your transaction

Your own journal and the provider's control plane can each be correct. The difficult history lies between their commits. A durable request key bridges that gap only if the remote endpoint assigns it the right semantics.

Worked example 4 · A promotion succeeds while its receipt disappears

Assume region A stores recovery intent R5 for standby S, with expected database role revision 11 and a stable provider request token P5. A dispatcher sends P5. The provider atomically accepts one promote operation P971 for that token, but the response is lost. A second dispatcher reads R5 after a claim expires. It should repeat the previously recorded request arguments, including P5, against the same token namespace. If the provider returns P971 it can persist the operation handle and poll for terminal state. A terminal success may still require checking the resulting database role and target binding before the workflow switches traffic.

Try a variation in which a cross-region endpoint offers idempotency only within its region. Sending the same bytes and token to region B now involves a different authority. If the provider cannot resolve A's history, B may create operation P112 independently. Route retries to the same authority, or use a strongly coordinated home region and make home-region unavailability an explicit blocking condition. Geographic recovery must preserve more than the client's key. It must preserve or safely rediscover the remote knowledge that gives that key its meaning. Document provider scope as precisely as tenant scope and operation scope.

Try a second variation. S has already been promoted with P5, immediately demoted through a separate administrative action, and is now again a standby. A naive reconciler checks only whether S currently has primary role. Seeing standby, it concludes the promotion never ran and requests a new promotion. State may cycle. Evidence about whether a historical action happened differs from evidence about whether a desired state holds now. Require an operation lookup, monotonic generation or append-only audit evidence when the historical fact matters. If the goal is ongoing convergence, re-evaluate the new desired generation and authority before attempting a new mutation.

A query by operation ID works when the worker managed to save the ID. It cannot recover an ID known only to a lost response. A search by a caller-supplied label is useful when labels are unambiguously bound at creation, enumeration is complete enough for the decision, and concurrent callers cannot create conflicting matches. An eventually consistent list returning zero results just after acceptance is weak evidence of absence. Querying it a second later reduces the frequency of mistakes but does not prove a bounded absence unless the provider promises one. For an irreversible operation with neither deduplication nor authoritative lookup, pause conflicting automation and surface the uncertainty.

Conditional operations and replay semantics

A compare-and-set provider can sometimes replace a dedicated token. Suppose a route update requires expected revision 11 and atomically advances it to 12. Even a duplicate delayed across a restart cannot apply that same revision-11 transition again, provided revisions are never reused in its valid lifetime. But a failed comparison on retry does not identify the winner. Another administrator might have advanced the revision first. If the caller needs a success receipt for its particular intent, store the intent identity in the resource transition or consult authoritative audit information. At-most-one transition under one precondition and discovery of its author are separate capabilities.

A stable natural resource name can also help. Creating an immutable artifact under a name derived from a digest allows concurrent identical writes to converge when the store guarantees the required conditional semantics. Verify that the existing artifact has the intended integrity, tenant isolation and policies before treating already exists as success. A user-chosen display name may refer to a previous installation with a different owner. Returning success merely because something of that name exists could give an attacker or another tenant's stale resource influence over recovery. Scope and validate the identity independently of whether a store returned an HTTP conflict.

Interview takeaway

I would put the two durable commits on the whiteboard and erase the line carrying the provider's response. Everything needed for recovery must remain derivable on the surviving sides. A provider-generated operation ID alone cannot be recovered from a lost first reply. A precommitted caller key plus a provider-supported repeat or strong lookup makes the mapping discoverable. Where that contract is missing, I would explicitly bound what automation can decide and give the operator evidence about unresolved effects before resuming.

SD-007 · Distributed Systems Foundations · 05

Outboxes, inboxes and the offset that actually counts

An event publisher, a broker and a consumer each have their own record of progress. Follow one quota reservation from its database transaction into a billing ledger and locate every point where an acknowledgment can disappear.

Worked example 5 · Two publications, one billed reservation

The reservation service commits reservation R44 and event E88 in the same database transaction. E88 lives in an outbox table with immutable semantic fields, a stable event ID, and the identity of its source aggregate. A relay reads E88, publishes it to a broker and crashes before marking it sent. The next relay publishes the same event again. The broker may allocate two different storage positions to those two publications. An application that deduplicates only by broker offset will consider them different. The stable event identity must survive the relay boundary; a randomly generated ID for each publish attempt defeats this design.

The billing consumer receives E88 at position 102. In one transaction it inserts E88 into an inbox scoped to the billing effect and debits the tenant's billing ledger. It commits and crashes before acknowledging position 102. On redelivery the unique inbox entry proves that this billing effect was already applied if it was inserted atomically with that debit. The consumer may acknowledge after the transaction has committed. When the second relay publication arrives at position 114 the same rule suppresses a second charge. The transport delivered work several times. Its customer-visible billing effect occurred once within the defined boundary.

Scope the inbox to the destination effect. A single upstream event might legitimately feed billing, search and an audit archive, each with a different processing lifecycle. A global seen-events table where the first handler suppresses all subsequent work can silently discard required effects. If one handler produces three ledger lines that must appear together, apply them under one transaction or make the unique identifiers for each component explicit and reconcile incomplete groups. Treating E88 as done after the first line and before the other two recreates the partial-commit defect inside the consumer.

A dead-letter queue adds another clock to the design. Operations staff may replay messages weeks after automatic retries expire. If the billing inbox deletes E88 after one day while a thirty-day dead-letter queue remains eligible for blind replay, a late message can produce a second charge. Either retain the authoritative effect identity for the longest permitted replay or make replay validate the durable business ledger and reject or quarantine expired identifiers. A short-lived metrics cache can report on recent duplicates; it cannot protect an unlimited recovery path. When designing retention, start with what the operator can actually redrive.

Stream transactions and external sinks

For a transformation fully contained in Kafka, a transaction can couple committed input offsets with output records written to Kafka topics. A consumer configured to read committed data observes the committed output rather than aborted transactional records. If processing fails before commit, the transaction can abort and the input can be reprocessed. The transformation code may execute more than once. The precise claim concerns the visible transaction result under the specified configuration. A handler that calls an external payment system during transformation adds a side effect outside that transaction. Aborting Kafka records cannot refund or erase an already accepted payment.

For a database sink there is another pattern. Within the destination store's transaction, write the derived records and advance a destination-owned input position. After a crash, resume from that durable destination position. Validate writer ownership on rebalance so a stale consumer cannot advance or overwrite a newer consumer's progress. This design can be efficient with strictly ordered per-partition processing. If input positions 100 and 102 finish but 101 is still outstanding, a single watermark of 102 falsely claims a contiguous prefix. Track gaps explicitly or release progress only through the largest truly contiguous committed prefix.

A business event may be republished with a distinct source message identity during disaster recovery. If the real invariant is one charge per reservation, making the billing table unique on reservation and charge type provides a stronger final defense than relying solely on delivery IDs. The relationship is subtle: several legitimate adjustments may apply to one reservation, so a single unique constraint on reservation alone could suppress a valid credit. Model original charges, reversals and later adjustments as separate named business intents with documented relationships. This is where understanding the business domain changes the correctness of the infrastructure.

Pause and trace the acknowledgments

With three colors mark event creation, broker confirmation and destination commit. Now remove the reply following each of those writes and trace what the next process knows. The exercise should produce possible duplicate relay sends and possible repeated consumer executions without a duplicate debit. If an unexplained arrow jumps from the database to the broker as if their writes succeed together, find the actual atomicity mechanism. The outbox trades a difficult atomic publish requirement for durable eventual dispatch, a known duplicate-delivery path and an explicit consumer obligation.

SD-007 · Distributed Systems Foundations · 06

Retry as a feedback controller

The individual request can be perfectly idempotent and its retries can still collapse the service. A retry policy regulates load on a dependency precisely when measurements are noisy and capacity may be falling.

Worked example 6 · A three-layer retry storm

A user-facing API calls a coordinator, which calls a storage adapter, which calls the provider. Suppose each of three retrying layers allows three attempts per invocation, including its first attempt. Under persistent failures on the deepest call, one top-level invocation can cause up to 27 provider calls if time budgets allow all branches. At 500 initial invocations per second, that is a worst-case attempted rate of 13,500 calls per second. This calculation assumes no coalescing, no shared budget and no earlier terminal exit. It is a bound under the stated schedule, not a prediction from average success rates.

Now say the provider can finish 1,000 calls per second when healthy. It has a transient pause and some callers time out after admission. Those callers submit retries before the provider has finished the original work. If duplicate recognition happens only after the expensive database lookup or execution begins, the dependency still pays considerable cost on every attempt. The overloaded queue lengthens service times, which creates more timeouts, which produces further retry traffic. Counting unique business effects after the incident can completely hide this wasted work. Measure attempt arrival, duplicate lookup cost, useful completions and backlog age independently.

Choose one layer to own retries for a given operation whenever practical. Propagate an end-to-end deadline and an explicit attempt budget across the other layers, including SDK defaults. Before starting an attempt, reserve enough time for the operation to have a realistic chance to finish and return a result. On a client with 230 milliseconds left, starting a call that normally needs 400 milliseconds is often just extra load. A server may still complete after the client gives up, so receiving a deadline exceeded result should trigger the ordinary ambiguous-outcome recovery path for mutations. Deadlines limit willingness to wait, not the historical existence of work.

Distribute retry arrivals with randomized delays. With full jitter a retry sleeps for a random duration from zero to a capped exponentially growing bound. This spreads a population of recovering callers across time. A per-client token bucket can restrict the amount of work spent on retries, while separate admission quotas can preserve capacity for first attempts or high-priority recovery. Honor provider retry hints subject to the remaining deadline and local service contract. If the next safe retry cannot fit, return a stable operation handle or explicit transient status instead of holding connections until every caller times out together.

Classify failure evidence before policy

Do not classify solely by HTTP status. A transport connection error after request transmission may be ambiguous. A provider response indicating invalid syntax can be definitive before execution. A conflict may mean the same key carries different arguments, the resource precondition is stale, another operation is in progress, or a transient serialization conflict should be retried. Define contract-specific categories and observability. Retrying a permanent fingerprint mismatch until the deadline wastes capacity and obscures an integration bug. Reissuing an unknown-outcome mutation with a new key because the old key returned a server error can cause a duplicate effect.

Hedged requests issue another attempt before the first one has timed out in order to reduce tail latency. For a safe read, hedging can help a caller escape one unusually slow replica if the system has spare capacity and the chosen replicas satisfy the required read consistency. For a mutation, simultaneous hedges require concurrent duplicate handling with a single authoritative decision. A cache that learns only after the first request completes leaves the overlapping window open. Even with correct deduplication, hedges create extra admission and lookup traffic. Set a small global hedge budget and measure the fraction of hedges that actually improve successful completion time.

PYTHON · executable model
def retry_fanout(attempt_limits):
    # All values include the initial transmission.
    deepest_calls = 1
    for limit in attempt_limits:
        if limit < 1:
            raise ValueError("at least one attempt is required")
        deepest_calls *= limit
    return deepest_calls

def drain_seconds(backlog, completions_per_second, new_per_second):
    spare = completions_per_second - new_per_second
    if backlog == 0:
        return 0
    return backlog / spare if spare > 0 else None

assert retry_fanout([3, 3, 3]) == 27
assert retry_fanout([1, 1, 3]) == 3
assert drain_seconds(60_000, 800, 600) == 300
assert drain_seconds(60_000, 600, 800) is None

Separate protection from progress

A bounded retry budget guarantees a limit on local attempt expenditure. It does not guarantee delivery within a particular deadline. A durable queue gives a request a place to survive caller departure, but its presence says little about time to processing while the dependency is unavailable. During an outage decide whether new work should be synchronously rejected, durably accepted up to a backlog cap, or delayed under explicit customer-visible policy. Estimate drain time as backlog divided by sustainable completion capacity minus new arrivals. If 60,000 unique operations are waiting, useful throughput is 800 per second and new arrivals are 600, the ideal drain time is five minutes. Counting 800 attempts as 800 completions during duplicate-heavy retries would make that estimate dangerously optimistic.

SD-007 · Distributed Systems Foundations · 07

Retain the evidence through expiry, repair and regional loss

Retention is an API guarantee coupled to operational behavior. An expired key can be harmless in one system and a direct path to duplicate billing in another.

Worked example 7 · A replay after the cache forgets

At 09:00 Monday client C submits key K to allocate a training slot. The allocation succeeds, but C is isolated before seeing the reply. On Tuesday the deduplication cache evicts K after a configured retention period. On Friday an operator restores the workflow from an older backup and replays activities it considers unfinished. If K is accepted as a fresh intent, a second allocation can appear. Nothing in the string K changed. The evidence used by the receiver changed. A retention policy must account for delayed messages, human redrive, backup restoration, asynchronous downstream retries and deliberate migration tooling.

Imagine keeping full response bodies for only a day, but retaining compact consumed-key tombstones longer. A tombstone can prevent another effect while allowing the detailed response to be reconstructed from the operation record or returned as expired. This trades replay fidelity against storage and privacy cost. If the system promises byte-for-byte replay forever, a compact record without those bytes cannot honor the promise. If the contract promises one reservation and an immutable operation reference, a compact binding may suffice. A client receiving expired or previously consumed must know whether it may safely reissue a new business intent.

A TTL cache by itself cannot distinguish an expired ID from one that has never appeared after it deletes every trace. To reject late requests reliably you need an independent validity signal, such as an authenticated time-limited submission token, an explicit bounded sequence protocol, or a durable business identity that outlives the cache. A timestamp supplied by an untrusted client can be useful for diagnostics but is weak enforcement. A snapshot restored to a time before tombstone creation can reopen a window that had been closed. Include identity evidence, business state and retained operations in restore design, then verify that their recovery epochs agree.

Size the store and the authority

Assume 2,000 new intents per second at sustained peak and a seven-day replay horizon. Seven days contain 604,800 seconds, so the service needs to account for 1,209,600,000 live keys. At 320 bytes of logical record data per key, that is 387,072,000,000 bytes, about 360.5 GiB before indexes, storage engine overhead, replication and backups. Three copies of the raw records alone require roughly 1.06 TiB. If a large cached response costs 32 KiB, returning immutable operation references can dramatically reduce the space occupied by retention. Use a distribution of response sizes and traffic by tenant when moving from this estimate to a procurement decision.

Index for the critical lookup by tenant, operation family and key. A time-ordered cleanup index can become a hotspot or generate bursts of tombstones and compaction. Time-bucketed partitions ease expiry, but a deduplication algorithm that must search every bucket for each request may become prohibitively expensive. An authenticated key carrying an issuance bucket can route lookup efficiently if that bucket remains valid under retries and clock rules. Alternatively use a stable hash partition plus asynchronous expiry. Keep outcome-authority routing stable even when service processes move. Replicating a read cache to many regions improves lookup latency but does not give independent writers a shared uniqueness constraint.

Consider an active-active failover design with asynchronous replication. Region A accepts K and performs the effect. Its request binding has not yet arrived in B when A becomes inaccessible. B receives K again. From B's local history this is indistinguishable from a first submission. A correct design can route K to its original surviving quorum, consult a downstream uniquely keyed business resource, or suspend uncertain requests until evidence is available. Granting B availability to perform all mutations while promising no duplicate irreversible effects despite missing evidence requires another authority the architecture has yet to name.

Handling a retention incident

When a production team discovers a misconfigured cleanup job, first bound the affected ranges of tenants, intent creation times and downstream operation types. Freeze automatic blind replay of the affected classes while permitting safe status reads. Reconstruct evidence where authoritative downstream ledgers support it. Avoid interpreting absence in a best-effort log aggregator as proof of no effect. Give operators a signed-off resolution for ambiguous items and a separate status for known duplicate effects requiring correction. Corrective actions need their own request identities; repeatedly refunding a duplicate debit can produce yet another business error.

Design an agent-assisted recovery execution gateway

An organization wants an agent to recommend recovery steps and a controller to execute approved failover plans across database, traffic and GPU capacity services. The gateway must survive restarts and give an operator a defensible answer to the question, “Did this action happen?”

Requirements and durable interfaces

Assume 20,000 active recovery groups, bursts of 5,000 accepted step requests per second during a regional incident, and provider operations ranging from a fraction of a second to forty minutes. A read-only rehearsal can tolerate duplicated computation. Executing a real promotion or acquiring paid capacity cannot silently produce an extra entitlement. Each accepted operation must survive loss of one availability zone within its home region. For a total regional outage, expose the independently established recovery point and the list of unresolved external effects before enabling conflicting writes. These are hypothetical design objectives, distinct from any vendor's service guarantees.

The agent proposes a structured plan naming resource identities, desired state, observed preconditions and the operation family. A user or configured policy approves a specific plan revision. The orchestrator persists an immutable step manifest containing stable child request identities. The model's later summary or regenerated tool arguments are insufficient to identify an approved effect. One model invocation may emit the same tool description twice, and a later invocation may assign a different tool-call ID while reconstructing essentially the same intent. Preserve the workflow-level identity across model retries, and require new approval if a material target or parameter changes.

The gateway offers SubmitStep, GetOperation and a reconciliation endpoint for appropriately authorized operators. The submission includes plan revision, step identity, target, expected resource generation and normalized desired action. The response distinguishes a durable accepted operation from a verified terminal outcome. The journal stores tenant-scoped key, fingerprint version, immutable plan metadata, current operation state and final result reference. The same transaction that admits a new request inserts an outbox row for dispatch. A unique constraint on the business key and a domain check on resource generation protect different dimensions of correctness. Deduplicating a stale but previously unseen request is insufficient to prevent it from changing the wrong generation.

Worked example 8 · Recover a branching plan with partial success

Plan 35 requires promotion of database D, preparation of a GPU service pool G and a routing change that depends on both. D and G have independently persisted step keys KD and KG. The dispatcher for D obtains provider operation 771, loses its journal update and dies. G completes, and its worker records verified capacity under KG. A second scheduler reads the plan. It should leave G's final outcome intact, recover D through its stable provider key, and keep route KR pending. Re-running the complete planner and deriving fresh child IDs from its new tool outputs could duplicate G and make the unresolved D operation impossible to recognize.

Suppose D's provider reports operation 771 succeeded. Verify actual readiness at the expected generation, then commit a transition that makes KR dispatchable only if both prerequisite outcomes and the approved plan revision still match. A stale executor could already hold a buffered route request from a previous recovery generation. The routing service must enforce a generation precondition or fencing token during its protected mutation. An immutable request key suppresses repeats of KR. A fence rejects operations from an obsolete owner or generation. Neither alone solves the other's problem. Keep one identity stable across worker replacement while revalidating currently authorized execution at dispatch.

After routing succeeds, assume the business wants a rollback. Record a separate compensation intent referencing the original successful route and a fresh expected revision. Replaying the original KR cannot perform the inverse action. The compensation may itself fail or become ambiguous, requiring precisely the same deduplication and recovery analysis. If a newer rollout has changed the route since plan 35, unconditional compensation could undo someone else's successful deployment. Expose the conflict to the plan owner and condition compensation on the state that is actually safe to reverse.

Capacity, observability and ownership

At a peak of 5,000 new intents per second, if 20 percent receive one duplicate transmission, the admission service handles 6,000 attempts per second while dispatching at most 5,000 distinct new intents per second before downstream replays. At an average logical journal entry of 1 KiB this is about 4.9 MiB per second of new admission payload, excluding index and replication traffic, terminal updates and outbox writes. If a provider accepts only 500 promotions per second, an admission burst dominated by promotions can build a backlog ten times faster than the provider drains new work while the burst persists. Shape admission per operation family, tenant and dependency.

Expose attempts per logical intent, mismatch rejection rate, missing-outcome lookups, time from accepted to verified, outbox oldest age, provider rate-limit responses and number of unresolved actions beyond the provider's supported replay horizon. A customer needs to know how much of the recovery plan can safely advance, not simply how many HTTP requests returned success. Store audit records of the approval revision, authority checked and evidence used to close each uncertain step. Avoid placing secrets, full prompt text or sensitive business payloads in tracing tags. Keep high-cardinality operation IDs in searchable logs or traces while publishing bounded metric dimensions.

For launch, pair each provider adapter with a capability matrix and an owner for its contract. One adapter supports durable request keys, another has a conditional version update and authoritative operation history, a third cannot reliably distinguish two identical actions across a timeout. Permit autonomous handling only to the extent supported by each adapter's evidence. For the third adapter, the workflow can record intent, handle unambiguous failures and expose a recovery task without asserting a result it cannot know. This preserves the useful portions of automation during a difficult incident.

Prove the effect before measuring the retry

Original interview practice. For each question, draw a durable state on the left and the external observation a customer could rely on to the right. Name the transaction or provider guarantee connecting them.

Twelve Senior Staff interview questions

The HTTP request timed out before returning an operation ID. Which identifier do you persist and when?

Evaluates crash-boundary reasoning. Persist the caller's logical action identity, canonical arguments and expected generation before dispatch. Require the provider to bind that identity durably to one operation and support replay or lookup. The issued operation ID is useful after acceptance but can disappear with the first reply. A replacement worker must be able to act in both the never-sent and accepted-with-lost-reply histories.

Follow-up. If the provider accepts keys only per region, recovery must respect that region's authority or supply separate evidence.

Common mistake and tradeoff. Reading a local pending field cannot distinguish two remote histories.

Why not hash the JSON body to deduplicate every call?

Evaluates intent and api contract. Two intentional requests for an identical reservation can have identical bodies. Conversely, optional defaults, representation order or trace metadata can differ across retries of the same intent. Give the caller a unique logical key, then use a versioned semantic fingerprint to detect incompatible reuse. A content hash is an appropriate identity for an immutable content-addressed artifact because its contract explicitly defines equality by content.

Follow-up. Capture chosen defaults if their effective values can change across versions.

Common mistake and tradeoff. Over-normalization merges different actions; under-normalization rejects harmless retries.

Can inserting the key into a cache before debiting an account ensure exactly one debit?

Evaluates atomicity of business mutations. If the insert succeeds and the process crashes before debit, later requests will suppress required work. If the debit happens first and the insert is lost, a retry can debit twice. Put the request binding and outcome in the debit's transaction or place an equivalent durable unique business identity in the ledger. A cache can accelerate lookups once its results are treated according to their consistency contract.

Follow-up. A durable admission record also needs recoverable ownership of unfinished work.

Common mistake and tradeoff. Avoid treating a marker whose only meaning is received as proof of completion.

Can you return the same successful response to a user whose access was revoked?

Evaluates security and replay fidelity. Perform authorization on replay just as for outcome lookup. The original operation may have succeeded even when a current caller may no longer see its results. Store immutable evidence and serve authorized response fields under the current policy. For signed download links, bind the durable outcome to an artifact and mint a new authorized link if the published API permits semantic equivalence.

Follow-up. Consider user deletion and response-body retention independently of narrow tombstone retention.

Common mistake and tradeoff. A global cache keyed solely by a caller-supplied UUID risks cross-tenant disclosure.

A single Kafka transaction writes output and advances its offset. Does this cover a remote tool call?

Evaluates transaction boundary. Only the configured Kafka transaction participants share the atomic commit. An external action may succeed before the Kafka transaction aborts. On replay the handler can call the tool again. Instead transactionally record a command with a durable identity, let a dispatcher perform it through an idempotent tool boundary, and reconcile uncertain outcomes. Downstream Kafka readers also need an isolation level consistent with the claimed visibility.

Follow-up. If the sink is a database, consider writing the destination position and output in one sink transaction.

Common mistake and tradeoff. Transactional transport and external execution each require their own proof.

How should an SDK respond when a known key returns the original terminal failure?

Evaluates failure classification. Check the published API contract and distinguish an authoritative recorded outcome from an ambiguous transport failure. Retrying the same key may be designed to return the same failure. To reevaluate a business action, first establish that the failed intent cannot still succeed, recheck state and authorization, then create a deliberate new intent if allowed. Unexpected key replacement inside generic retry logic can undermine the safeguard.

Follow-up. A locally observed server error alone may leave the remote effect unknown.

Common mistake and tradeoff. A caller needs separate operations for recover prior result and request new work.

Can you prune keys after 24 hours if automated clients retry for only ten minutes?

Evaluates recovery horizons. Enumerate queue retention, client restarts, backup restoration, human redrive, cross-region catch-up and the lifetime of external operations. A ten-minute online retry budget says little about replay after a week-long disaster. Keep business identity longer, reject expired tokens using independent evidence, or restrict replay with reconciliation. A deleted record cannot by itself tell new intent from expired intent.

Follow-up. What if response storage is expensive? Keep a smaller durable binding and reconstruct authorized outcome references.

Common mistake and tradeoff. A TTL is a product guarantee when it determines whether execution may recur.

Two regions each have a healthy copy of the deduplication table. May both admit a write while disconnected?

Evaluates authority under partitions. If replication is asynchronous, the two copies can each observe absence and accept the same key. A regional unique index only constrains that region. Assign each key a single available authority, use a cross-region quorum that preserves uniqueness, or push the uniqueness check to an authoritative downstream resource. A region lacking required history can serve safe reads or queue pending intent under explicit semantics.

Follow-up. Measure the availability and latency cost for the selected assignment and recovery policy.

Common mistake and tradeoff. A copy being healthy says nothing about whether it has seen a concurrent commit elsewhere.

How does a consumer safely advance progress when partition entries finish out of order?

Evaluates ordering and recovery. Use per-message durable identities or maintain a committed prefix and an explicit gap structure. Advancing the watermark to the largest completed offset can skip lower unfinished messages after a crash. Persist progress with the associated sink effects and fence obsolete workers after a rebalance. If business order matters, even correct duplicate suppression cannot repair reordered state transitions without a separate ordering rule.

Follow-up. Parallelism can be organized by independent resource key if the invariants permit it.

Common mistake and tradeoff. Treating the largest seen offset as the largest contiguous applied prefix loses work.

What metric tells you whether retries help during overload?

Evaluates capacity reasoning. Measure logical completions per second and client-observed time to verified effect, then relate them to attempt rate, queue age and duplicate handling cost. A higher HTTP success count might simply mean more replayed admission responses. Compare controlled cohorts or failure drills with bounded retry budgets. Check tail behavior after recovery because synchronized backoffs can create repeated overload waves.

Follow-up. Track the oldest unresolved high-priority operation separately from aggregate throughput.

Common mistake and tradeoff. A high dedup hit rate might indicate healthy recovery or an uncontrolled retry storm.

Can the tool-use ID from an LLM replace the workflow's payment key?

Evaluates agent execution identity. It normally identifies a particular tool request in a model interaction. A regenerated plan may produce a fresh tool identifier for the same approved payment, and identical tool arguments could also describe another legitimate payment. Bind the durable effect identity to the approved business intent and validated parameters. Preserve the mapping to tool call IDs for tracing and format the result for each interaction as required.

Follow-up. Who may alter the amount? Require a new authorized decision instead of silently reusing the old identity.

Common mistake and tradeoff. The model's ability to describe an action gives it no authority to repeat a committed effect.

How would you prove the service is safe enough to deploy?

Evaluates test design and limits. Construct schedules around every durable boundary, including a lost first response, overlapping requests, two distinct keys racing on capacity, lower-version recovery and expired evidence. Assert a business invariant over the full effect history and check the returned mapping for each client. Inject failures into the actual database and provider integration where feasible. Pair the safety result with measured recovery time under stated assumptions.

Follow-up. Can the test prove all schedules safe? Its coverage is finite; document the protocol argument and test scope.

Common mistake and tradeoff. A dashboard with a plausible final count can hide duplicate effects followed by cleanup.

Worked example 9 · Crash the test harness at each boundary

Start with a ledger at ten units and a pending request to reserve six. Make the test harness stop after writing the request binding but before a shared transaction commits. Restart the service, retry the same key and check there is exactly one six-unit reservation and four free units. Repeat at the point immediately after commit but before the caller receives a reply. The same invariant must hold in this second history. Now submit two truly distinct keys, each requesting six units. At most one should be entitled to the capacity, demonstrating that identity checks and quota checks are independently effective.

For an external service, replace the provider with a deterministic fake that owns its own durable log. Schedule acceptance of operation 771, hold the response and crash the caller. Have the successor resubmit the original provider key while the first response is still buffered. Deliver responses in each order. Inspect the provider log for actual newly created operations, not just a final dashboard value that could hide two creations followed by cleanup. Simulate expiration of the provider key and restoration of an older client snapshot. In that final branch the expected result may be a blocked uncertainty state. A test that requires every branch to return success encourages unsafe assumptions.

A production readiness review

Ask a colleague unfamiliar with the system to find a stale operation using only the documented status API, traces and provider audit evidence. Give them a task where the first successful provider outcome was later superseded by a different administrator. Check whether they can distinguish original success from current desired state. Time this exercise under simulated regional degradation and record which dependency limits the recovery. A good operational design makes the remaining uncertainty visible and prevents an operator from accidentally starting a fresh irreversible action while investigating the old one.

Speak through a retry-safe design

A clear answer moves from a single lost reply to a complete service boundary. Use one concrete request identity throughout and change only one assumption at a time.

Thirty seconds · Explain idempotent effects

A retry repeats the transport attempt for an existing business intent. I give that intent a stable identity before dispatch. The service atomically binds the identity to the protected mutation and its result, so a retry can recover the earlier outcome. When the mutation happens in a separate provider, that provider must honor the same identity or expose an equivalent conditional operation and recovery mechanism. A timeout leaves the outcome unknown.

Two minutes · The failover worker loses its operation ID

Consider a standby promotion. The worker can crash just before it sends the request or just after the provider accepts it. Both failures leave an empty operation ID in the worker's database. Always retrying can create a duplicate in the second history. Always skipping can omit the promotion in the first. I therefore commit the logical step identity, target and expected generation before calling the provider. The provider accepts a caller-supplied token, rejects incompatible reuse and lets me recover the same accepted operation on replay.

The replacement worker uses that original token. It resolves the operation handle, waits for terminal completion and verifies the resulting database generation before moving traffic. I distinguish a successful submission from a successful promotion because a unique operation may still fail. I also distinguish deduplication from fencing: a new unique command from a stale controller can still be unsafe. The resource needs an authority or generation check at the mutation boundary.

The limits matter operationally. If the provider remembers keys for a shorter period than our disaster recovery process can replay work, a sufficiently old retry becomes ambiguous again. My adapter keeps a capability record for key scope, retention, lookup semantics and external preconditions. When that evidence is insufficient, the operator sees an unresolved step and the rest of the plan can proceed only along explicitly independent branches.

Five minutes · Defend exactly-once effects

I would begin by specifying the effect a customer can observe. For a training reservation it might be one active entitlement and one charge per approved allocation intent. Several HTTP attempts, queue deliveries and speculative scheduling computations can occur. They must converge on that single business result. I would also specify the failure domain, replay horizon and what progress is promised when the authoritative region is unreachable.

The submitter records one logical identity before the first transmission. The receiving service authenticates the caller and scopes the key by tenant and operation family. It binds the key to versioned canonical arguments. Two callers using the same key for incompatible quantities receive a conflict. Two different keys with identical quantities can represent two valid independent purchases. A tracing identifier is useful for investigating a single transmission but is usually the wrong identity for the business operation.

For a local quota reservation, I place the request binding, capacity check, entitlement and outcome in one transaction. An outbox written in that transaction records the obligation to publish an event. If the relay crashes after publishing, it may send the same event twice. Billing keeps an inbox identity or business uniqueness constraint in the same transactional domain as the charge. It acknowledges a message only after its effect is durable. With these boundaries I can show precisely where an acknowledgment can disappear and why the financial output remains valid.

I then test the weakest boundary. If the reservation dispatches an external GPU allocation, my database cannot make that call part of its commit by writing an extra status field. The provider must deduplicate my stable child key or enforce an equivalent resource precondition. I reconstruct an unknown outcome using the original identity and verify the actual resource. If I have neither downstream idempotency nor authoritative evidence of the earlier effect, immediate automatic repetition cannot give a hard exclusion guarantee. The product must decide whether temporary unavailability or possible duplicate allocation is the acceptable cost.

Finally I protect liveness. I assign retries to one layer, use an end-to-end budget with jitter, and measure attempts per successful logical intent. I test lost replies, overlapping retries, stale replicas, expired tombstones, restored backups and provider timeouts during regional handoff. My acceptance test checks the full effect ledger and the elapsed time until an operator can safely advance. This lets a reviewer challenge a named assumption and see exactly which promise would change if that assumption fails.

Final interview cheat sheet

  • Intent. A caller-approved decision to perform one business action.
  • Attempt. One transmission of an intent; trace each attempt separately.
  • Effect. Specify the customer-visible mutation and its enforcement point.
  • Fingerprint. Validate the meaning of repeated keys under versioned argument semantics.
  • Scope. The authority can enforce uniqueness only inside its documented namespace.
  • Atomicity. Record the protected mutation and its durable identity together.
  • Outbox. Commit the obligation to publish with the originating business transition.
  • Inbox. Commit suppression evidence with the destination effect.
  • Horizon. Keep evidence through every permitted replay path.
  • Reconciliation. Resolve ambiguous outcomes using authoritative downstream evidence.
  • Budget. Cap retry work using propagated deadlines, limits and jitter.
  • Generation. Reject stale but individually unique actions at the resource boundary.

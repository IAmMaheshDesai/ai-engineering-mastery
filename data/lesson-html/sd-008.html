SD-008 · Distributed Systems Foundations · 01

Start with what the customer may observe

At 10:04 a research team receives confirmation that its eight-GPU job has been admitted. The scheduler owns eight devices, the budget service still shows no reservation, and the cluster is starting the processes. Which of those facts constitutes a broken promise?

Worked example 1 · Write the acceptance contract

Give the tenant a budget of one hundred credits and a job an estimated cost of sixty. The fleet has exactly eight free GPUs that meet the requested topology. Job J1 and job J2 arrive together. Both eligibility checks pass when they independently read the same free capacity and balance. If each scheduler calls a billing service after launching, both jobs may receive a successful admission while only one can run or pay. Even an impeccably reliable message broker cannot repair that mistake. The admissibility predicate was evaluated before either caller acquired an exclusive claim to the resources on which it relied.

Define a success response that operations staff can verify. One possible contract says that accepted means the service has durably recorded an intent, and the job might still fail admission. Another says that admitted means the tenant has an enforceable credit hold and an exclusive capacity reservation with compatible validity intervals. Running should mean the selected workers actually report the expected attempt as ready. A client needs these distinctions to decide whether to launch a duplicate, wait, cancel or contact support. Returning a job ID immediately is reasonable under the first contract. Describing that response as guaranteed capacity silently imposes the second contract without acquiring the resources necessary to support it.

Represent the two quantities as independent resources with explicit owners. The budget owner must keep total active holds and committed debits within its limit under concurrency. The fleet owner must prevent overlapping reservations of the same physical capacity and must fence obsolete attempts at the place that accepts launches. The workflow owner must avoid declaring a job admitted until it has suitable evidence from both. These are separate invariants with different enforcement mechanisms. Cross-service orchestration becomes easier once each participant rejects its own impossible transitions atomically instead of trusting a remote coordinator's observations.

Draw the smallest possible transaction

Consider moving budget and capacity metadata into a single transactional store. A command checks the available budget and matching free capacity, creates both reservations, and records the admission result in one database transaction. A transaction that observes a concurrent change may need to restart under the database's isolation rules. A unique request key lets the caller retrieve its outcome after a network failure. Launching GPU processes remains outside this transaction. This alternative solves a significant part of the original problem with fewer moving parts. Splitting logically related metadata by team ownership can turn one straightforward conditional update into a distributed recovery protocol.

There are reasons to retain separate systems. The fleet inventory may belong to a global allocator serving several products, while credit policy is managed under a different release cycle and access boundary. Real GPU devices cannot enlist their running processes in a general database transaction. Ask which boundaries are operational choices and which are physical facts. A distributed commit protocol is plausible when every participant can prepare durable tentative changes and later honor a shared decision. When a participant exposes only ordinary create and cancel operations, the design has to work with committed intermediate states. A saga is one way to make those states explicit and recoverable.

How I would explain this in an interview

I would start by naming the promise that a successful admission makes. A request ID can mean the platform accepted a command for processing. A capacity guarantee requires exclusive claims that survive the advertised failures. A running job additionally requires evidence from the device agents. I would preserve each invariant where its data lives, then decide which promises need one atomic decision and which can tolerate a visible intermediate state. That decision determines whether a local transaction, distributed commit or compensating workflow fits the operation.

SD-008 · Distributed Systems Foundations · 02

Two-phase commit as a durable promise

One database owns the credit ledger and another owns a reservation calendar. Both support prepared transactions. We need both local changes to commit or both to abort, including after either database or its coordinator restarts.

Worked example 2 · Follow transaction T64

Start with a durable transaction identity T64 and a fixed participant list containing Budget and Calendar. Each database executes its local tentative change under a concurrency-control method adequate for its invariant. When asked to prepare, Budget verifies it can hold sixty credits and persists the information required to commit even after its process dies. Calendar validates its slot and does the same for the reservation. A participant that votes yes has made an obligation. It can no longer unilaterally abandon the proposal just because its application connection disappeared. A participant that cannot safely make that obligation votes no.

The coordinator collects the votes. If any required participant votes no, a commit decision is disallowed. When all required participants have prepared, the coordinator can choose commit according to the protocol. It durably records the decision before informing any participant that the transaction committed. Budget may receive the decision immediately, install its local committed state and acknowledge. Calendar may receive it later, even after a restart. Replaying the final commit for the same transaction identity should converge on the recorded outcome. The decision becoming irrevocable and every participant having applied it are separate points in time.

Observe the information held by Calendar after it sends yes but before it receives the final decision. It knows that its own local work can commit. It has no evidence that Budget managed to prepare. Even receiving a message saying that Budget prepared would leave the coordinator's decision and recovery rules relevant. Now compare this with Calendar before it makes a durable yes promise. There are circumstances when a participant still in its initial state can abort locally under the protocol. The moment of the durable affirmative vote changes what unilateral recovery is permitted. This is why logging after sending the vote is a correctness bug.

Identify the decision record

The transaction ID must be unique for the full recovery and retry lifetime. Reusing yesterday's ID risks interpreting a late commit message as an order for today's unrelated reservation. A recovery record names the participant set, global identity, decision and configuration or protocol version needed to interpret it. Participant records need sufficient undo or redo information, plus whatever locks or conflict evidence support their promises. A replicated database normally supplies these details internally. When reviewing a proposed application implementation, ask what actually survives a simultaneous process crash and machine replacement. A mutex or in-memory dictionary cannot stand in for a prepare record.

A phase-two acknowledgment is particularly important for log retention. An optimized protocol may forget records under specific conditions, but simply deleting old transaction outcomes after a fixed short interval can make a returning participant unable to distinguish commit from abort. Establish the recovery and garbage-collection contract before choosing a retention period. The coordinator might keep decisions until every participant confirms, maintain a durable high-water mechanism, or use a documented presumed-outcome protocol with carefully specified logging assumptions. The absence of a row in an arbitrary backup is never sufficient proof that a transaction aborted.

def choose(votes, required):
    # A coordinator model before the irrevocable decision.
    if any(votes.get(p) == "no" for p in required):
        return "abort"
    if all(votes.get(p) == "yes" for p in required):

    return "waiting"

Estimate latency with the right dependency graph

In an illustrative sequential design, assume local work has finished and prepare calls run in parallel. Budget replies after seven milliseconds including durable prepare, while Calendar replies after twelve. The coordinator's decision flush then costs four milliseconds, and phase-two delivery plus durable application takes five and nine milliseconds respectively. If the client contract waits for both participants to acknowledge, these stages take twelve plus four plus nine, or twenty-five milliseconds, before client transit. A contract that returns after the decision is durably recorded may have a shorter response path. Reads must still honor the promised visibility semantics while participants catch up.

Do not add the latency of every replica and participant as though all work ran serially. Conversely, do not assume every step overlaps. A participant cannot safely learn a positive decision before the protocol has made that decision durable. Increasing the participant count can push up the time of the slowest prepare, the amount of state held during a stall and the likelihood of conflict. An answer that quotes two network rounds but omits persistence, locking and recovery paths has estimated messages, not the customer's latency.

SD-008 · Distributed Systems Foundations · 03

Recover the decision without guessing

A coordinator disappears while one participant is prepared. The on-call engineer has an abort button and a five-minute recovery objective. The first task is to identify the action that could turn a recoverable outage into inconsistent data.

Worked example 3 · Two histories look identical

In history A, both Budget and Calendar prepare. The coordinator durably decides commit, tells Budget, and fails before contacting Calendar. Budget now has a committed credit hold. In history B, Calendar prepares, Budget votes no, and the coordinator durably decides abort before failing. Calendar's local state is identical in both histories. It has a prepared record and no decision message. If Calendar times out and rolls back, history A has a split outcome. If Calendar commits on timeout, history B has a split outcome. Waiting or locating authoritative evidence is the only generally safe option given that local information.

A real recovery protocol may obtain a definitive answer from an informed participant or recover the coordinator's log. A participant that knows it committed can communicate an outcome under the system's trust and protocol assumptions. A collection of peers that all report prepared need not have enough information to terminate. This is the classic blocking case for ordinary two-phase commit. Availability depends on which evidence disappeared and whether the system can recover it. Saying that any coordinator failure always blocks the whole database overstates the result. Usually the affected uncertainty and locks are scoped to specific transactions and conflicting work.

Now imagine the coordinator is a replicated state machine. Its durable decision can survive a failed host, and a successor can replay the same outcome from the replicated history. This improves resilience against the loss of a coordinator process. It does not authorize a commit after a mandatory participant voted no or failed before preparing. A quorum of coordinator replicas agrees about the coordinator's state. Each prepared participant contributes a different logical vote about its own work. Counting three coordinator replicas as three consenting resource managers confuses two different axes of replication.

A recovery procedure for a prepared database

In PostgreSQL a prepared transaction can survive the issuing session and continue holding locks. A repair procedure inventories prepared identities, checks which business service created them, and looks up the authoritative outcome using a trusted transaction manager. Verify that the lookup covers the correct deployment lineage. An identical transaction identifier from a test environment, previous cluster or restored coordinator can be actively misleading. Only execute the matching finalization operation once the decision and affected participant are established under the service's recovery contract. Treat any forced heuristic decision as a separate incident that requires reconciliation.

Suppose a dashboard reports 120 prepared transactions with a median age of four seconds and an oldest age of two hours. The median is a poor signal for the blocking risk. One old reservation can hold a popular tenant row or capacity index and prevent a large queue of otherwise healthy admissions from completing. Inspect the lock wait graph, affected tenants, transaction age distribution and decision lookup errors. Gate new work on already hot keys if needed to avoid turning a bounded incident into widespread retry traffic. Requeueing every blocked command on a different application server does nothing to release the database dependency.

Atomic commitment and isolation answer different questions

Even when the final commit-or-abort outcome is globally consistent, a client doing two independent reads can see a changing world. A report might read Budget before T64 commits and Calendar afterward. The two statements did not request a single consistent snapshot. Similarly, a poor isolation level can allow two transactions to make individually legal local decisions whose combined outcome violates a cross-row condition. Atomic commitment settles which writes take effect together under the transaction system's semantics. Serializable isolation constrains the concurrent histories the system may expose. State the read contract and isolation level required by the invariant before claiming an application cannot observe partial or anomalous results.

SD-008 · Distributed Systems Foundations · 04

Break long work into semantic transactions

Preparing a database for twenty minutes while operators investigate a failed network switch ties unrelated work to the duration of a human decision. A saga can release local resources earlier if the business can live with the intermediate states it exposes.

Worked example 4 · Reserve, stage, then activate

Consider a training job that first reserves sixty credits, then reserves an eight-GPU slice, then stages an immutable model artifact, and finally sends a launch command. Each local step commits independently. Once the credit hold commits, another job sees fewer credits available. If the GPU reservation fails, the workflow releases precisely the credit hold it created. Another observer may have seen reduced availability in the meantime and decided to schedule a cheaper job. Compensation cannot recreate the alternative history in which that observer acted differently. The promised outcome is a valid business correction, with the intermediate effects acknowledged in the contract.

Name each forward action and its semantic counterpart. Reserving funds pairs with releasing an unconsumed hold. Consuming credits pairs with a compensating ledger entry under a refund policy. Staging a content-addressed artifact might require no immediate inverse if sharing and later garbage collection are safe. Launching a process may require a scoped, authenticated cancellation and confirmation that the relevant device is actually reusable. These differences mean that automatically calling a method named undo on every successful activity is a poor workflow abstraction. A compensator is an application operation with its own preconditions, durable result and failure modes.

The original saga formulation addresses a sequence of local transactions that can interleave with other transactions, with compensations to amend partial execution. Modern services often add message queues, external providers and a workflow engine. These additions require explicit assumptions that a database alone might otherwise provide. For example, a provider may accept a request, lose its reply and publish an eventually consistent status later. The orchestration layer needs a way to determine whether a step succeeded before deciding that an inverse should run. A chain of local transactions cannot automatically grant reversibility to an external operation whose outcome is unknown.

Pick a pivot and document its consequences

A useful design exercise is to find the business point after which backward recovery is no longer acceptable. For a failover, beginning to accept new customer writes in the recovery region can be such a pivot. Once those writes exist, simply pointing traffic back at the previous primary risks losing or diverging them. Treating the route change as its own reversible API call ignores the application history behind that route. A safer design specifies how to complete forward, isolate the old primary and, if needed, perform a separately planned failback that accounts for data synchronization.

The pivot is a policy and domain boundary, not a special instruction in every saga runtime. Some workflows have several irreversible consequences. Others can be modeled with a series of short-lived reservations followed by a narrow publication operation. Move expensive validation ahead of the pivot where feasible and make post-pivot work repeatable. Keep irreversible work off the failure-prone path when a staged alternative provides equivalent value. For example, write and verify a candidate routing configuration before publishing a pointer to it. Staging reduces risk but still needs access control and a cleanup policy for abandoned candidates.

Describe failure in the user's language

A job that could not launch might end in declined with reservation released, failed with refund pending, or blocked awaiting device reconciliation. Those statuses have different implications for a second submission. Returning a generic failed result encourages a client to retry as though no resources were consumed. A useful operation resource explains what the system knows, which claims remain live, and whether retrying with the same request identity is safe. Keep forward progress, repair progress and the immutable reason for the original failure independently observable. A repaired saga should not erase the fact that it initially failed at the provider boundary.

SD-008 · Distributed Systems Foundations · 05

Compensate the claim you actually own

An operator sees a job that briefly reserved six GPU slots and then failed. Restoring the inventory row to its value from before the job ran is tempting. Another customer's reservation makes that action dangerous.

Worked example 5 · The lost update inside an undo

A pool has ten free devices. Job A reserves six and the available count falls to four. Job B reserves three of the remaining devices and the count falls to one. A then cancels. Its compensator reads the saved original count of ten and writes it back. The resulting reported availability exceeds physical availability by three. If a third job relies on that value, the accounting bug becomes a real double allocation. The stored before-image was true when A began, but the local transaction at B made a legitimate change after that point. Compensating A must preserve B's contribution.

Create reservation rows keyed by stable reservation ID, tenant, pool and attempt. Each row has quantity, state and the version that guards authorized transitions. A release of A changes A's active claim to released under a conditional update. If an aggregate counter is maintained, increment availability by six in the same local transaction as that transition. A duplicate release finds that A is already released and returns its recorded outcome. If the reservation was converted to occupied capacity, the correct inverse may require first draining the job and verifying release at the node. A crediting operation based solely on the count can make unusable GPUs appear available.

Simulate a timeout immediately after the local release commits. The workflow does not know whether the counter rose, and the retry worker sees a new message delivery. Executing an unconditional increment a second time grants another six fictitious slots. Binding release to the transition from active to released makes the duplicate answer recoverable. Also bind the request to the immutable original quantity. A duplicated release request with a different quantity or tenant should produce a conflict or authorization failure. A debugging endpoint that permits arbitrary manual release by number can bypass every invariant the normal service path enforces.

Order inverses by dependencies

The simplest sequential saga compensates completed steps in reverse order. That order is a helpful starting point when later effects depend on earlier resources. Suppose a process runs on a GPU reservation and publishes a heartbeat through a service identity. Releasing the reservation before halting or fencing that process may allow a different tenant onto a still-occupied device. Removing the only identity capable of issuing the shutdown before the shutdown completes can make cleanup impossible. Build a compensation dependency graph from actual resource relationships, and include any credentials or operation handles needed by the repair path.

Independent compensators can run concurrently when their effects commute and neither relies on the other's resource. A failed model rollout might delete an unshared staging prefix and release a detached budget hold in parallel. It cannot delete a checkpoint still referenced by another training job just because the failed rollout uploaded its first copy. Use ownership records, reference checks, durable pins or versioned immutable identities appropriate to the underlying store. If the referenced objects are subject to eventual listing or garbage collection, base deletion eligibility on authoritative evidence and a retention contract rather than an empty cached listing.

PYTHON · executable model
def release(free, holds, claim):
    # Model one atomic local transition, including duplicate handling.
    remaining = dict(holds)
    quantity = remaining.pop(claim, 0)
    return free + quantity, remaining

free, active = release(1, {"A": 6, "B": 3}, "A")
assert (free, active) == (7, {"B": 3})
assert release(free, active, "A") == (7, {"B": 3})

Control what other transactions can infer

Releasing database locks between saga steps improves concurrency, but concurrent services can react to data that may later be compensated. Mark a reservation pending and teach the consumers of that reservation which transitions are allowed. A payment service might prohibit spending held credits while a user interface still reports them separately from finalized debits. A model registry can distinguish a staged version from the version authorized for serving. Calling this a semantic lock means the application's state and transition rules enforce a temporary restriction. Every path that affects the protected invariant must honor the restriction, including maintenance jobs and administrative overrides.

There is a cost to being explicit. Pending resources can accumulate if workers stall. A background expirer that simply frees anything older than five minutes may release a live claim during an unusually slow launch. Give reservations a documented expiry contract, make consumption compare the validity and generation atomically, and define how an uncertain in-flight launch is reconciled before hardware is reused. A blind timer is appropriate for some purely logical holds. The same timer is unsafe when the real resource can continue acting after the database declares it free.

SD-008 · Distributed Systems Foundations · 06

Deliver commands across a crash boundary

A saga can have theoretically perfect compensators and still charge a tenant twice. Examine the short interval between a participant committing its work and its caller learning what happened.

Worked example 6 · Outbox, inbox and unknown outcomes

A workflow row says reservation required for job J. In one local database transaction, the coordinator changes the step to requested and appends an outbox record with message K, tenant T, step version three and a hash of the significant parameters. If it crashes before commit, neither change exists. If it crashes after commit, a relay can discover the record. The relay sends the command to Budget and crashes before marking it delivered. Another relay sends K again. The outbox has solved the atomic recording of intent and pending publication. It has deliberately left open the possibility of duplicate delivery.

Budget processes K by checking a tenant-scoped inbox identity, validating the payload and atomically creating the credit hold plus the response record. Two consumers may race. A transactional uniqueness constraint or equivalent conditional write must arbitrate them, and the loser must distinguish an existing matching completed request from an in-progress or conflicting one. A separate read saying no prior key exists followed by an unconditional debit is insufficient under concurrency. Budget publishes a result using its own outbox or serves it through GetReservation. A repeating result message is harmless if the workflow accepts only its expected step identity and transition version.

Now insert a separate GPU provider behind the local participant. The participant cannot atomically commit its inbox row with a vendor's remote launch unless the vendor offers a shared transaction protocol. Marking the inbox completed before dispatch risks losing the launch. Dispatching first and then marking completed risks issuing the launch twice after a crash. Record durable intent before dispatch and use a provider-supported stable request key, a queryable unique resource identity or a documented reconciliation process. If two distinct launches would be dangerous and the provider cannot disambiguate the first, suspend conflicting automation until enough evidence is available.

Decide whether an error warrants compensation

An explicit, definitive rejection of a launch tells the orchestrator something different from a socket timeout. In the first case the provider contract may establish that no workload was created. In the second case it may be running. If a timeout triggers immediate release of both budget and capacity, the workflow can leave a live unpaid job occupying devices it now advertises to others. Enter an unknown state with the original request identity, provider region, observation timestamps and any returned handle. Retry an identical request only within the provider's documented deduplication scope and retention interval.

A late success during compensation is another independent race. Suppose a cancel request changes a saga from launching to cancel requested. The provider then reports that the launch completed before cancellation took effect. Ignoring the late result could orphan a real workload. Accept the observation as evidence about the provider, then evaluate the current desired state. If the desired outcome is canceled, dispatch a scoped shutdown against the exact job attempt and keep the reservation until termination is verified. Do not allow a stale event handler to move the top-level saga back to running merely because the event accurately describes an earlier provider transition.

Preserve the identity hierarchy

A customer retry, a forward step, a compensation and a transport delivery each need appropriate identities. A payment capture and a refund should have separate request identities even when they share one saga ID. Otherwise a provider may interpret the refund as a conflicting reuse of the capture key. Conversely, a transport retry of the same refund should reuse its original key. Keep a link to the original resource and business reason. These relationships support replay, dispute investigation and tenant-specific auditing without requiring a production engineer to infer semantics from a wall-clock timestamp.

Retention is a correctness parameter. Suppose the workflow can reappear after seven days but the external provider remembers request keys for only twenty-four hours. Automatic replay on day three can create a second effect. Keep the external reference, restrict late retries, negotiate longer provider retention or design a durable business key discoverable even after transport deduplication expires. Document the behavior clients can expect when support restores old jobs or replays a dead-letter queue. Background repair that silently bypasses the public retry contract is often the most dangerous caller.

SD-008 · Distributed Systems Foundations · 07

Design a recoverable regional failover service

A hypothetical disaster recovery product coordinates a database, network policy and an inference fleet. Customers want a clear recovery point, a known writer, and enough serving capacity before traffic shifts. The components expose different transaction capabilities.

Worked example 7 · Walk a recovery group through a failed cutover

A customer requests failover for group G, expecting configuration generation 12. The system already has a replicated checkpoint in the destination. The primary may still be reachable by some clients, so loss of coordinator connectivity alone cannot authorize a second writer. The API authenticates the tenant, binds a request key to the desired operation and atomically records the workflow plus the expected group generation. Competing requests use a conditional generation fence. The response identifies an accepted operation. It makes no claim yet about recovered traffic, database writability or the destination checkpoint's exact recovery point.

A phase of parallel preparations verifies the checkpoint's lineage, reserves destination capacity, stages an immutable route and validates credentials. Each action records its reference, result and compensation policy. The next hard prerequisite is a provider-enforced barrier that prevents the previous primary from accepting conflicting writes under the stated failure model. If the provider cannot establish that barrier during a partition, the ordinary workflow must stop or narrow its contract. An emergency human decision to accept possible divergence is a different business operation with explicit authorization, audit history and a reconciliation plan. It cannot be disguised as a successful normal failover.

Imagine the destination database promotion succeeds but the route publication times out. The workflow must determine whether the old route, new route or a mixture is actually serving traffic according to the routing provider's semantics. Meanwhile determine whether any new writes reached the promoted database. If so, automatic reversal can strand acknowledged writes. Set desired outcome to completion at the new site and reconcile the route through its versioned API. If the route provider supports only asynchronous propagation, report the configured revision separately from verified reachability and observed traffic. A local status row saying published provides insufficient evidence of end-user recovery.

Components, persistent records and boundaries

An API admits authenticated requests and exposes status by operation ID. A replicated workflow store records desired outcome, state version, participant references, retry deadlines, irreversible actions and evidence gathered by verifiers. Workers claim steps under expiring leases, while the affected providers enforce request identity and authority at their mutation boundaries. A command relay drains durable intents; an observation service records provider outcomes without allowing stale callbacks to rewrite current intent. A reconciler schedules uncertain and expired steps for review. Separate metrics distinguish admitted requests, authorized mutations and actual end-user recovery.

Keep a per-group record for the invariant that at most one conflicting promotion may proceed in the normal control plane. Consider one local transaction that checks expected configuration generation, ensures the group has no incompatible active operation, records the new operation, and emits the first outbox entry. The workflow can later mark another operation as eligible only under the documented terminal or supersession rules. A superseding workflow cannot simply erase an earlier unknown provider call. It must inherit its unresolved effects or prove they were drained. This local transaction is a natural place for strict atomicity even though the overall recovery plan is a saga.

Define the participant evidence contract in data terms. A checkpoint verifier records source timeline, restore point, manifest digest and the destination's validated restore readiness. A capacity reservation records tenant, resource IDs, epoch and consumed state. A database adapter records provider operation ID, target lineage and observed primary status. A route change records expected prior revision, proposed revision and subsequent observation. Persisting these records supports diagnosis when operators rotate, regions restart or the workflow binary changes. A single text field containing the most recent provider error is woefully inadequate for determining safe compensation.

Quantify recovery, load and risk

Assume 600 groups need recovery after one region loses power. Each group performs four independent checks and nine mutating steps, and each mutation produces at least one durable intent and one outcome record. The minimum workload is 7,800 step invocations and 10,800 mutation-related durable records, before retries, heartbeats or polling. If the slowest shared provider admits 20 promotions per minute, completing one promotion for all 600 groups requires at least 30 minutes at that limit. Adding a thousand workflow workers cannot overcome that bound. Prioritize customers and reserve provider capacity according to explicit recovery objectives.

For one group, suppose checkpoint validation takes eight minutes, capacity preparation takes eleven, and credential and routing validation take two. Run independent preparations in parallel and the optimistic preparation time is eleven minutes. Fencing the primary requires another three minutes, promotion five, and verified route convergence two. A serial dependency chain after the preparation gives an optimistic twenty-one-minute recovery path, excluding queueing, retries and operator decisions. A proposed fifteen-minute objective now requires changing a dependency, pre-establishing readiness, purchasing more capacity or weakening what the time metric counts. An honest design identifies the exact assumption that must change.

Security, evolution and the architecture defense

Limit worker authority to the tenant, resource and action it is executing. A compensation worker often runs long after the calling user's interactive credentials expire. It needs an auditable service authorization tied to the originally approved repair scope, not a reusable copy of a tenant-wide token. Protect emergency promotion and deletion with distinct privileges. Logs should carry operation identity and provenance without exposing sensitive credentials or unrestricted provider payloads. Unauthorized compensation can be as damaging as unauthorized forward execution because it may withdraw capacity or reverse a legitimate customer effect.

Version the stored plan and its interpretation. A new release might split a historical promote step into fence, promote and verify. A workflow already waiting for the old step's outcome must reconcile using its original identity and semantics. A migration should preserve what was already sent, what could still act and which historical compensator applies. Introduce shadow validation or new-version admission by cohort before broad migration. In a design defense I would test a simultaneous software rollout, coordinator crash and delayed provider callback. The system should recover the same customer intent and avoid issuing a second promotion, even when a fresh plan would have organized the work differently.

SD-008 · Distributed Systems Foundations · 08

Test the dangerous schedules and manage repair debt

A transaction service can meet its average latency objective while a few prepared participants block the same tenant for hours. A saga engine can show excellent task completion while irreversible external outcomes remain unverified.

Worked example 8 · Reproduce a cutover that should pause

Use a deterministic provider model with distinct accepted, applied and observable states. Seed it with a primary in region A, checkpoint 400 at region B and an immutable request to promote B under generation 13. First establish the writer barrier at A. Dispatch promotion to B, apply it at the provider, then drop the reply before the workflow stores an operation handle. Deliver the user's cancellation while the workflow still says promotion pending. A valid implementation must account for the possibility that B is already active. It must not conclude that promotion never happened and re-enable the old writer merely because the user canceled.

Fork the execution. In one branch a provider lookup definitively reports that promotion completed and a new commit was accepted at B. Drive forward repair or the explicitly authorized recovery policy while maintaining exclusivity. In the other branch the provider definitively proves the original promotion cannot still execute, and the source can be safely reactivated under a new barrier consistent with the product contract. These branches have different admissible next actions. A useful test asserts which effects must remain impossible in each. A test that checks only whether the final state eventually reaches a word like completed or canceled can pass while the system violated exclusivity along the way.

Add duplicated compensation replies, an expired worker lease, and an old success event arriving after a newer plan revision. For local records verify that accepted transitions are monotonic within the defined state machine. For external resources verify that every accepted mutation names an authorized attempt and every terminal declaration has supporting evidence. A deterministic model explores these schedules cheaply. Then run selected schedules with real storage persistence barriers and provider sandbox contracts. A mock that always makes its status endpoint immediately consistent can hide the production ambiguity the exercise was designed to reveal.

Keep a repair queue you can actually drain

Treat unresolved compensations as an inventory with arrival and service rates. Suppose 1,200 launches per hour each have a two percent probability of reaching a repair queue. That produces 24 repair items per hour on average. If two operators together resolve 20 items per hour, the queue grows by four every hour even on an uneventful day. An outage can produce a burst much larger than the average. Estimate time to drain after service returns and reserve enough automatic repair capacity for common, evidence-rich cases. Human review should focus on cases where the action really depends on additional authority or missing evidence.

Track the age and value of unresolved holds, the number of unknown effects, customer impact by stage and availability of the participant's authoritative lookup. Separate a prepared database transaction from a compensated but still visible business correction. A prepared transaction can hold database locks. A compensated operation can release application resources but still require communication to a customer who saw a provisional result. A single failed jobs metric conflates them. Make high-risk repair commands idempotent, auditable and scoped to a particular case, and give operators a read-only explanation before allowing a mutation.

How to argue for a simpler architecture

If a prototype accumulates dozens of cross-service compensations for every customer request, revisit the data ownership model. Consolidating strongly coupled facts under one authority can eliminate entire classes of ambiguity. Another option is to change the business interaction from immediate assignment to explicit reservation followed by confirmation, moving uncertainty into a bounded customer-visible state. A third is to preallocate finite quota to partitions so regional decisions remain local within a safe bound. Preallocation can strand capacity and requires a trustworthy transfer protocol. Every simplification should name the new assumption, customer impact and operational cost.

Interview studio · defend a transaction boundary

The following are original practice questions. For each one, identify the decision maker, the evidence needed after a crash, and the customer-visible outcome the proposed recovery path would preserve.

Twelve Senior Staff questions

A participant voted yes an hour ago. Can it roll back to unblock a tenant?

Evaluates atomic commitment under uncertainty. Its affirmative prepare is a durable obligation. Another participant may already have applied a commit decision. I would locate the transaction manager's authoritative decision or use the system's documented termination protocol. Local age is evidence of an incident, not an outcome. Protect the hot key from additional load while recovering the evidence.

Follow-up. What if its local application connection died? The prepared transaction remains a database concern and can outlive that session.

Common mistake and tradeoff. A manual heuristic rollback can create a divergent business result and requires explicit reconciliation.

Does replacing the coordinator with a Raft group remove the need for every resource manager to prepare?

Evaluates protocol composition. The group can replicate the coordinator's decision and survive some operator or machine failures. Each distinct resource manager still has to affirm that its part can commit. A voting replica of the coordinator cannot promise capacity owned by an unavailable resource manager. I would specify the failure assumptions for each participant group and for the decision record separately.

Follow-up. Could a majority of participants commit? Only under a different business contract in which missing participants need not contribute to the atomic operation.

Common mistake and tradeoff. Increasing coordinator replication can improve availability while increasing persistence and networking costs.

Can a system have atomic commits but still produce a misleading cross-service report?

Evaluates isolation and read contracts. A reporter performing independent reads can read one resource before a transaction and another afterward. Even a globally atomic decision does not turn two unrelated API calls into one snapshot. Define which snapshot or revision the report needs and whether the participating services support it. If the report is eventually reconciled, show its observation watermark and avoid using it for strict admission decisions.

Follow-up. Would a transaction automatically prevent write skew? That depends on its isolation and predicates.

Common mistake and tradeoff. Name the anomaly and required read contract before choosing coordination.

A compensator sets free GPUs back to ten after job A fails. What counterexample do you draw?

Evaluates concurrent compensation. Give A six GPUs and B three of the remaining four. Restoring ten implies B owns none. I would condition a release on A's specific active reservation, credit its exact quantity once and retain B's reservation. If A had already launched a workload, I would additionally verify device release before readmitting those GPUs.

Follow-up. What if compensation itself times out? Reconcile or repeat the same conditional release with its own stable command identity.

Common mistake and tradeoff. A before-image captures a historical value, not current authority over every change since then.

Should cancellation always run the workflow's completed steps backward?

Evaluates domain-specific recovery. First determine the irreversible boundary and actual completed effects. After a new database primary accepted writes, pointing clients back at the former primary may lose acknowledged history. Some effects are safely compensable, some can remain harmlessly staged, and others require forward recovery. Order actions by their resource and authorization dependencies. Record unresolved effects until their outcomes are known.

Follow-up. Can two compensators run concurrently? Yes when their effects commute and neither removes a resource or credential the other needs.

Common mistake and tradeoff. Reverse order is a candidate ordering for a sequential saga, never proof of business safety.

Your outbox publisher sends a message and then crashes. Which property survived?

Evaluates durable intent and duplicate handling. The local business change and pending publication were atomically recorded. The broker may have received the message before the publisher could mark it delivered. A relay can therefore send it again. The consumer needs a stable command identity and an atomic decision that binds deduplication to its local business effect. A further external call introduces another boundary with its own recovery contract.

Follow-up. What if the consumer writes the inbox and crashes before calling a provider? A separate pending effect and reconciliation mechanism must drive the provider operation.

Common mistake and tradeoff. Describing the outbox alone as end-to-end exactly once omits the effect boundary.

Can a launch timeout immediately trigger a refund?

Evaluates unknown outcomes. A timeout leaves open both no launch and successful launch with a lost response. Refunding while the job is active can violate billing and capacity invariants. Query the original operation or retry its stable identity within the provider contract. If the desired state is canceled, stop and verify the exact job attempt before releasing its device claim. Refund policy may separately allow an intentional goodwill credit.

Follow-up. What if the provider has no lookup or deduplication? Suspend conflicting actions or redesign admission around a stronger boundary.

Common mistake and tradeoff. Do not encode a commercial exception as technical proof that an external effect never happened.

How would you keep 600 simultaneous failovers from overwhelming a database provider?

Evaluates bottleneck and recovery planning. Estimate shared provider throughput and the number of required calls per group. At twenty promotions per minute, 600 required promotions occupy at least thirty minutes. Use fair bounded queues and verified priority classes, stage independent readiness checks ahead of the bottleneck and bound retries. Show customer-facing estimated wait separately from active promotion duration.

Follow-up. Would dynamic worker scaling improve the bound? It helps worker-bound stages but cannot increase the provider's admission contract.

Common mistake and tradeoff. Aggressive polling can use the same limited capacity needed to finish useful work.

Should every service publish its recovery events independently, or should one orchestrator own the plan?

Evaluates ownership and observability. Independent events suit a small number of stable participants with clear local obligations and few cross-step decisions. For a complex failover with pivots, uncertain provider outcomes and partial cancellation, a durable orchestrator makes the owning decision and repair policy explicit. It still relies on participants enforcing their local invariants. I would track the execution plan version and monotonic state transitions regardless of topology.

Follow-up. What is the centralization cost? The orchestration state store and scheduler need sharding, recovery and capacity planning.

Common mistake and tradeoff. A message bus can distribute communication without assigning responsibility for resolving a stuck case.

How would you migrate an active saga to a changed plan?

Evaluates durable version compatibility. Preserve the meanings and identities of effects already issued. A pending legacy promotion cannot simply be replaced with a new multi-step sequence that starts by sending a second promotion. Continue the old version or migrate through a proven mapping that transfers evidence, outstanding operations, compensation obligations and authorization. Test delayed old callbacks after the migration point.

Follow-up. What about completed workflows? Retain enough historical interpretation to support audits, deduplication and authorized late repair.

Common mistake and tradeoff. Dropping old code too soon can turn a routine restart into an unrecoverable business obligation.

How do you cap risk when compensation requires a human?

Evaluates operational capacity. Estimate the arrival rate of exceptional cases, the value tied up per case and the team's actual drain capacity. Route deterministic cases to automated repair with idempotent scoped commands. Escalate ambiguous or irreversible cases with the exact evidence and decision required. If arrivals exceed service rate, use admission controls or change the participant contract before backlog becomes normal.

Follow-up. What should page the on-call engineer? Oldest high-impact unresolved case and loss of the authoritative lookup are stronger signals than raw retry counts.

Common mistake and tradeoff. An operator queue is a designed service with an availability and capacity budget.

Where would you deliberately place a strong database transaction inside a saga?

Evaluates invariant-driven boundaries. I would combine the group generation check, new operation record and outbox intent in one local transaction. In a participant, I would combine an idempotency result with the precise local credit or reservation transition. These boundaries prevent local races and make crash recovery straightforward. The cross-provider process can remain a saga where participants expose only committed operations.

Follow-up. When reconsider the service boundaries? When most operations require distributed agreement over facts that can be owned and served together.

Common mistake and tradeoff. Local ACID transactions and sagas often solve complementary parts of the same production problem.

Whiteboard and failure analysis exercise

Draw three columns for the coordinator, a database with prepare support, and a remote provider that accepts only create and cancel. Explain the design aloud under pressure

Choose the shortest explanation that still identifies the invariant. Use the longer explanation when an interviewer asks you to defend behavior during a crash or a regional outage.

Thirty seconds · Why compensation matters

A distributed commit protocol can make several participating databases agree on one commit-or-abort decision when each can durably prepare. A saga handles longer work by committing smaller steps and recording how to amend their business effects. A compensator must identify the exact claim it owns, preserve other customers' concurrent changes and survive retries. When a provider times out, I reconcile the original operation before releasing anything that a live workload might still be using.

Two minutes · From prepared to uncertain

I would distinguish the uncertainty of a prepared database from the uncertainty of an external API caller. A database that voted yes knows it can complete its local change. It may have to wait because it cannot tell whether the global transaction committed. The authoritative transaction decision resolves that uncertainty. Once every mandatory participant is prepared, the coordinator can durably decide to commit and disseminate the outcome. A participant that guesses abort because its timer expired can disagree with another participant that already applied commit.

A workflow caller that times out after issuing a launch has a different problem. It may have no idea whether the provider accepted the request in the first place. Recording intent before dispatch and using a stable request key lets a successor query or safely retry under the provider's contract. If the workflow compensates a related credit hold immediately, it might give the customer a running job with no charge. I need evidence about the actual effect, a durable scope for compensation and a policy for conflicts that cannot be resolved automatically.

I would first ask whether the business invariant can live in one local transaction. Where it cannot, I would preserve the strongest required invariants at their owners and expose accepted, pending, verified and repair states to the user. Then I can explain what happens on the most inconvenient crash without hiding data loss or double allocation behind a successful workflow response.

Five-minute architecture rehearsal

Let me ground this in a regional recovery service. The customer wants a safe primary and working traffic, so a success message from the orchestration database is not the business outcome. I would define three milestones. We can accept an intent under a group generation, authorize the specific promotion after checking its prerequisites, and finally verify recovery at the destination. The operation API records which milestone has actually been achieved. A request timeout can be resolved by looking up the original request rather than racing a second promotion.

I put the generation check, creation of the workflow record and publication intent in one local transaction. That prevents two controllers from independently authorizing conflicting normal operations. The workflow then stages capacity, verifies the checkpoint and prepares a route. These steps produce stable references that survive a worker failure. Each local reservation has its own transition rules and compensation. Releasing an old reservation changes the claim created by this workflow. It never restores a global counter to a snapshot taken before other customers acted.

Next I ask how the old primary is fenced. If both sites can still accept conflicting writes, all the bookkeeping in the workflow engine will not restore a coherent database history. I require an enforceable barrier and preserve evidence that it took effect. I send promotion with a recoverable identity, and I expect ambiguous completion as a normal intermediate state. If the response disappears, a successor reconciles the exact provider operation. It does not automatically move traffic back or re-enable the old primary. Those actions have to account for writes the destination may already have acknowledged.

Scale affects the recovery plan as much as correctness. A hundreds-of-groups regional outage can saturate the provider that admits promotions even when orchestration workers are idle. I would separate tenant fairness, provider rate limits and queue age from the duration of one successful step. I would measure time to safe database availability and time to verified customer traffic. A high workflow completion rate means little if customers remain pointed at the previous site or if manual repair cannot drain its queue.

Finally I would test the cutover by withholding the promotion reply and injecting cancellation at that instant. I would observe the database and router, not just the workflow status. My strongest defense is being able to say exactly what evidence allows the next action. Where the provider cannot tell us whether a prior action may still complete, I expose the blocked state and protect the invariant while we reconcile. If the business wants a faster default, we can then discuss the specific API improvement or explicit risk acceptance that would change the design.

Final interview cheat sheet

  • Atomic commitment. All participants obey one transaction outcome.
  • Prepared. A durable affirmative vote creates an obligation across restart.
  • Decision record. Outcome knowledge must survive coordinator replacement.
  • Isolation. Concurrent reads and writes need a separately defined contract.
  • Saga. Locally committed steps may interleave with other work.
  • Compensator. Amend the owned business effect under current conditions.
  • Pivot. Choose forward recovery after a domain-specific irreversible boundary.
  • Semantic lock. Enforce temporary domain restrictions in every relevant writer.
  • Outbox. Persist local change and publication intent together.
  • Inbox. Bind repeated command identity to one local effect and result.
  • Unknown outcome. Recover evidence before releasing resources or reversing direction.
  • Repair debt. Track unresolved value, case age and capacity to drain the backlog.
